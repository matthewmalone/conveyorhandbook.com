1. Belt Splicing

Splices are the weakest section of a conveyor belt. The belt carcass construction determines which joint methods are permitted.

3. Hot Vulcanised Stepped Splices (Multi-Ply Fabric)

Multi-ply fabric belts (EP, PN and NN) are joined with a stepped splice of N - 1 steps, where N is the number of plies, vulcanised under heat and pressure.

Table 2: Hot Vulcanised Stepped Splice Sequence
Step Action Critical Controls Defect if Uncontrolled

Step length set by the strength of each ply, to the belt manufacturer's splice instructions.A square splice concentrates dynamic stress at pulleys, idlers and cleaners. Short steps reduce bonded area.
2. Strip and stepAt each step line, cut through the cover and the ply being removed, then peel that ply from the skim rubber. Use induction cover heaters and stripping winches where available.Do not score the ply beneathA cut into an underlying ply reduces its strength across the full splice
3. Buff and cleanBuff all bonding surfaces to a uniform texture. Clean with the manufacturer's solvent, typically ethyl acetate or methyl ethyl ketone (MEK).Do not overheat the rubber. Do not use degreasers, mineral spirits or unfiltered compressed air. Control ignition sources, as both solvents are flammable. Substrate temperature at least 3 deg C [5 deg F] above the dew point.Scorched or contaminated surfaces bond poorly. Condensation vaporises during cure and leaves voids.

4. Cold Vulcanised Stepped Splices (Multi-Ply Fabric)

Cold splicing uses the stepped splice geometry bonded with two-part polychloroprene cement at ambient temperature, without a press. Use it on lower-tension fabric belts within the cold-bond system manufacturer's rating.

Table 3: Cold Vulcanised Stepped Splice Sequence
Step Action Critical Controls Defect if Uncontrolled
1. Mark out and stepMark the bias and step lines, cut and peel the pliesBias 0.3 or 0.4 times belt width. Step length set by ply strength. Do not score the ply beneath.Damaged plies and reduced bonded area
2. BuffBuff all bonding surfaces to a uniform textureWithout heat and pressure, adhesion depends on mechanical keyWeak bond
3. Clean and check conditionsClean with the manufacturer's solventSubstrate temperature at least 3 deg C [5 deg F] above the dew point. Confirm cement compatibility with heat-resistant cover compounds.Condensation or an incompatible compound reduces adhesion
4. CementMix the base cement and hardener and apply to both surfacesUse within the manufacturer's open time and expiry dateAged cement bonds poorly
5. AssembleJoin the steps, roll out entrapped air and rebuild the covers. Apply weight over the splice during cure.Uniform contact across the full spliceEntrapped air and unbonded areas
6. CureHold the conveyor stopped through the cure periodTypically 4 h for a usable cure and 24 h for full cure, depending on cement formulation and ambient temperature. Apply the manufacturer's cure time.Loading before cure shears the bond

Note: Cold splices have lower heat resistance than hot vulcanised splices and degrade faster on hot materials such as cement clinker, foundry sand and hot ash.

5. Finger Splices

Finger splices join belts that cannot be stepped: solid woven PVC and PVG, single-ply straight-warp and aramid belts. They also join some high-tension multi-ply fabric designs.

Table 4: Finger Splice Sequence
Step Action Critical Controls Defect if Uncontrolled
1. Mark outMark the fingers with steel templates. Set the splice square or on a bias as the belt manufacturer specifies.Finger length to the belt manufacturer's splice instructionsShort fingers reduce shear area and joint strength
2. Cut fingersCut with heavy-duty saws or pneumatic punchesDo not cut fingers by handIrregular gaps between fingers disrupt tension transfer
3. Prepare surfacesBuff and clean the finger edges for vulcanised splicesSubstrate temperature at least 3 deg C [5 deg F] above the dew pointPoor adhesion and voids
4. Fill and interlockPVC: apply plastisol or jointing paste between the fingers. PVG, straight-warp and aramid: apply tie-gum. Interlock the fingers.Fill every finger gapUnfilled gaps carry no shear
5. CureCure the full splice in a single press settingTemperature, pressure and time to the manufacturer's cure tablePlastisol or rubber flow and finger displacement at a press overlap
6. Cool and releaseHold full press pressure until the joint cools below 70 deg C [158 deg F]. Water-cooled platens shorten the cooling time.Confirm release temperature at the platensWeak, porous joint
7. Finish and inspectTrim flash. Check finger alignment and joint thickness.Joint thickness matches the parent beltUneven joint strikes cleaners

6. Steel Cord Splices

Steel cord belts are joined only by hot vulcanised overlap splices. Use the belt manufacturer's splice design to ISO 15236-4 (formerly DIN 22131-4).

Table 5: Steel Cord Splice Sequence
Step Action Critical Controls Defect if Uncontrolled
1. Align and markOverlap the belt ends, align them on the true belt centreline and mark the biasBias 0.3 times belt width or 0.4 times, unless the splice design specifies a square spliceA misaligned splice mistracks
2. Strip and clean cordsStrip the covers with motorised cord strippers and induction cover heaters. Clean with the manufacturer's solvent.Hold the blade inclined toward the cover to leave a core rubber film on each cord. Do not buff or score through the zinc coating. Substrate temperature at least 3 deg C [5 deg F] above the dew point.Low cord-to-rubber adhesion
3. Cut and lay cordsCut the cords to the lengths in the splice diagram and lay them in the step patternStaggered cord ends. Number of steps and step length to the splice design. Step count rises with cord diameter relative to cord pitch. Use alignment combs to hold the cords parallel.Coinciding cord ends concentrate stress. Misaligned cords.
4. Insert core rubberLay core rubber strips (noodles) or pre-grooved core rubber panels between the cordsMinimum gap approximately 0.25 times the cord diameter. Typically one third of the cord diameter.Cord-to-cord contact removes the shear path
5. Build edges and coversBuild the edges with edge rubber and apply the cover rubberConsolidate the edges fullyExposed outer cords corrode inward (zipper failure)
6. CureCure the full splice in a single press setting. Tighten traverse beams with calibrated torque wrenches in a set sequence.Temperature, pressure and time to the manufacturer's cure table, loggedRubber flow and cord displacement at press overlaps. Voids.
7. Cool and releaseHold full press pressure until the splice cools to approximately 70 deg C [158 deg F], or the manufacturer's release temperatureDo not tension the belt until the press is removedBlistering and porosity
8. InspectInspect cord geometry by radiography (X-ray) or MFL scanCord alignment and core rubber gap to the splice design. Reject any cord-to-cord contact.Geometry errors reduce static and dynamic splice strength

7. High Integrity Splicing (HIS)

Apply HIS to high-tension steel cord belts and to belts where splice failure has a high production or safety consequence. HIS adds engineering controls to the steel cord splicing process to reduce variation in field splice quality.

Table 9: High Integrity Splicing Controls
Control Requirement Purpose
Splicing enclosurePressurised enclosure with heating, ventilation and air conditioning (HVAC)Holds temperature and humidity within the splice material manufacturer's limits. Absorbed moisture vaporises during cure and causes voids.
Cord alignmentLaser alignment jigs and machined aluminium combs over the full splice length, which can reach 4 to 5 m [13 to 16 ft]Keeps the cords parallel and on the splice pattern
Core rubberPre-grooved core rubber panels in place of hand-laid stripsHolds the design gap between cords
Cure controlDigital proportional-integral-derivative (PID) controllers, multi-zone thermocouples and data logging in place of manual gaugesControls and records the full cure profile against the target
Baseline scanRecord a radiograph or MFL scan of every splice after cureSets the baseline for condition monitoring

8. Splice Inspection and Repair

Splices degrade through dynamic fatigue, impact damage and chemical breakdown. Inspect splices routinely by visual, tactile and auditory checks to identify degradation before the belt parts.

8.1 Splice Inspection

Transverse crackingTransverse cracks at the leading or trailing splice endsAbrupt stiffness transition between belt and splice. Pulley diameter below the belt minimum. Square or short splice.Spot repair cover cracks before moisture enters. Check pulley diameters against the belt manufacturer's minimums. Replace the splice where cracks reach the carcass.

Edge contact with the structure from mistracking. Poorly consolidated edge rubber.Correct belt tracking. Rebuild the edge where cords or plies are intact. Replace the splice where cords are broken or corroded.
Step edge liftingLifted cover or ply edge along a step line, visible as a ridgeContaminated or under-buffed bond line. Cleaner blade catching the step edge.Re-bond the lifted edge with tie-gum under a spot press. Check cleaner blade angle and tension.
Finger tip separationFinger tips lifting or cracking along the finger boundariesIncomplete fusion or bond. Fingers too short. Press released before cooling.Spot repair isolated tips. Replace the splice where separation extends along the fingers.

Table 11: Splice Repair Methods
Method Description Application Limitations

Grinding flushGrind proud splice rubber flush with the beltThumping and cleaner impact from a thick spliceDoes not correct cupping caused by internal separation
Mechanical fastener reinforcementFit solid plate fasteners across a delaminating fabric splice edge to bind the pliesShort-term contingency on fabric belts onlyDo not use on steel cord belts, where fasteners can be ejected under tension
Temporary fastened closureCut out the failed splice and close the belt with mechanical fastenersEmergency return to service on fabric and solid woven belts, within the fastener limits for the beltDerate the working tension.
