1. Belt Capacities and Material Properties

Belt capacity is defined by volume, not mass. Size the belt so that the design capacity fills its full cross-section.

2. Factors Governing Belt Capacity

Operating close to the full cross-sectional profile maximises power efficiency (fixed belt, idler and drive losses spread across maximum payload) and improves tracking, as a centred load stabilises the belt. Belt capacity depends on four interrelated factors:

  • Belt width and edge clearance: set by required capacity and material lump size.
  • Belt speed: set by material characteristics (dustiness, abrasiveness, degradation) and loading and discharge arrangements.
  • Idler configuration: troughing angle (20, 35 or 45 deg) and roll count (3, 4 or 5-roll).
  • Material properties: bulk density, angle of repose and surcharge angle.

3. Properties of Bulk Materials

3.1 Angle of Repose versus Surcharge Angle

  • Angle of repose: the angle a pile forms when material is deposited onto a stationary flat surface (static state).
  • Surcharge angle: the angle material settles into while being vibrated and transported on a moving belt (dynamic state). It is typically 5 to 15 deg less than the angle of repose, and up to 20 deg less for some materials. Aerated powders exceed this offset.

The maximum incline is typically 15 to 18 deg for dry bulk materials. Exceeding this limit induces material rollback. Free surface water lubricates the belt interface and reduces the allowable incline. Moisture without free surface water increases cohesion and can permit a steeper incline, as for damp sand. Inclines above 20 deg typically require moulded cleats, chevrons or corrugated sidewall belts.

4. Maximum Lump Sizes and Particle Ratios

Maximum lump size, the largest a belt can carry without excessive spillage or carcass damage, depends on belt width and whether lumps are uniform or mixed with fines. Conveyor Equipment Manufacturers Association (CEMA) practice sets the following limits:

  • Uniform lumps: For screened, predominantly uniform material (e.g. ballast rock), limit lump size to one fifth of belt width at a 20 deg surcharge angle, or one sixth at a 30 deg surcharge angle.
  • Mixed lumps: For unscreened material such as ROM containing approximately 90% fines, lump size can reach one third of belt width. The fines cushion and bed the lumps into the profile centre, mitigating edge migration.

5. Typical Belt Speeds

Material degradation, dust generation and transfer chute geometry limit belt speed.

  • Dusty materials: limit to 2.5 m/s [500 fpm] to reduce windage stripping of fines from the belt.
  • Fragile materials: limit to 2.0 m/s [400 fpm] to minimise material degradation at transfer points (e.g. sized coal, wood pellets).

Overland conveyors handling ROM coal, copper or iron ore typically operate at 5.0 to 8.0 m/s [1,000 to 1,600 fpm]. These speeds require engineered transfer chutes (hood and spoon) to match material trajectory velocity to the receiving belt speed.

6. Volumetric Cross-Section and Edge Clearance

Material loaded onto a troughed belt forms a cross-section comprising a trapezoidal base (material within the trough) topped by a circular segment (the surcharge). Maintain an edge clearance, c, at each belt edge to limit spillage. DIN 22101 and CEMA define the minimum edge clearance for standard bulk materials.

Note: The formula applies to belt widths up to 2,000 mm [79 in]. Above this width, DIN 22101 sets c at 125 mm [5 in].

The standard formulas assume a homogeneous, relatively fine bulk material. For large ROM lumps or primary crusher discharge, the standard edge clearance is often insufficient.

Large lumps do not flow as a homogeneous mass. They rest on the material profile and migrate toward the edges during idler transit. A lump whose dimension exceeds the edge clearance can roll off the belt edge.

For ROM and coarse lump material, increase the edge clearance above the standard value so that the wing idler slope contains lump migration. This reduces the usable volumetric capacity of the belt. Recalculate capacity with the increased clearance.

7. Effect of Troughing Angle on Capacity

The troughing idler angle dictates the cross-sectional area. Increasing the troughing angle from 20 deg to 35 or 45 deg increases volumetric capacity and can permit a narrower belt for the same throughput, reducing structural capital cost.

A 45 deg trough imposes higher bending stress at the idler junctions and requires a carcass rated for the trough angle. It also requires longer transition distances at the terminal pulleys to limit edge tension and fatigue. Deep troughing also restricts maximum lump size. Large lumps can wedge in the deep trough profile, jamming the loading chute or gouging the belt cover.

9. Design Capacity versus Nominal Capacity (Surge Handling)

Upstream flow is rarely uniform. Do not design a conveyor to operate continuously at 100% of calculated volumetric capacity.

Nominal capacity is the average feed rate required per operating hour.

Design (peak) capacity is the maximum surge rate the conveyor must handle without spillage or drivetrain overload. Design capacity is typically 115% to 125% of nominal capacity.

11. Increasing Capacity by Belt Speed

Where mass capacity must increase on a conveyor already at full volumetric capacity, a wider belt is the direct solution, but the structural replacement is frequently capital-prohibitive. Increasing belt speed by gear ratio change or variable frequency drive (VFD) raises mass throughput at constant cross-section. Verify each component in the table below before modification.
