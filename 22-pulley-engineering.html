1. Pulley Engineering

This chapter sets out minimum pulley diameters, pulley geometry, lagging selection, tension transfer and the structural design of shafts, shells, end-discs and bearings. Design formulas for these checks close the chapter.

2. Pulley Diameters

Pulley diameter governs belt bending fatigue life and depends on carcass thickness, carcass modulus, operating tension and wrap angle. An undersized pulley overstrains the outer fibres to fracture and compresses the inner fibres, causing crinkling and delamination.

2.1 ISO 3684 Pulley Classifications (Types A, B and C)

ISO 3684 sets out a method for determining minimum pulley diameters. Pulleys are classified into three types according to belt tension zone and wrap angle. ISO 3684 values are minimums for use where belt manufacturer data is unavailable.

The standard does not apply to carcasses thicker than 20 mm [0.79 in], belts with intermediate rubber or plastic layers thicker than 0.8 mm [0.03 in] between plies, hot materials above 100 deg C [212 deg F] or ambient temperatures below minus 40 deg C [minus 40 deg F].

Where a belt is rated above the duty (over-belting), the tension ratio, defined as maximum belt tension at the pulley divided by rated working tension, falls, and ISO 3684 permits smaller pulley diameters. Reduce Type A, B and C diameters by one standard step for tension ratios of 30% to 60%, and by two steps for tension ratios less than 30%. The tables below include this shift for each tension range.

Imperial values are nominal pulley sizes, not exact conversions. Some are up to 5% smaller than the exact conversion, e.g. 630 mm converts to 24.8 in against a nominal 24 in. For imperial designs, select a pulley not smaller than the exact conversion of the metric minimum.

3. Pulley Geometry

3.1 Parallel Face Pulleys Versus Crowned Pulleys

Specify parallel face pulleys for steel cord, aramid and straight-warp belts, and for all Type A pulleys.

A belt running onto a crown steers toward the larger diameter, centring the belt. This centring effect requires sufficient unsupported belt lead-in length to permit lateral bending of the belt. High-modulus and steel cord belts lack the transverse flexibility to conform to crown profiles.

Crowned pulleys increase tension in the centre warp yarns or steel cords and reduce edge tension, accelerating carcass and splice fatigue.

Limit crowned pulleys to bucket elevators and to low-tension tail and take-up pulleys on low-modulus plied fabric belts with adequate lead-in length. Where crowning is required, calculate crown geometry to maintain centreline tension below the carcass fatigue limit. Apply the minimum pulley diameter at the smallest diameter of the crowned face.

3.2 Pulley Face Width

Specify a pulley face width greater than the belt width to accommodate transverse wander and reduce edge damage. Typical face width allowances are:

  • Belt width up to 650 mm [26 in]: belt width plus 100 mm [4 in]
  • Belt width greater than 650 mm and up to 1,400 mm [26 to 55 in]: belt width plus 150 mm [6 in]
  • Belt width greater than 1,400 mm [55 in]: belt width plus 200 mm [8 in]

Increase these allowances by 50 mm [2 in] for conveyors subject to structural movement or lateral wind load, e.g. underground coal mines, floating pontoons and overland conveyors.

3.3 Pulley Balancing

Unbalanced rotating mass generates centrifugal force that causes vibration, bearing fatigue, structural fatigue of the conveyor frame and non-uniform lagging wear. Balance all pulleys and check runout before commissioning.

4. Pulley Lagging

Pulley lagging is a sacrificial, high-friction layer bonded to the steel pulley shell that resists macroscopic slip on drives, sheds water and dirt, and protects the shell from abrasive wear. On drive pulleys, a higher friction coefficient lowers the minimum slack side tension (T2) and belt tension, which can reduce the required carcass strength.

4.2 Ceramic Tile Configurations

Specify ceramic lagging according to whether the pulley drives the belt or only redirects it.

  • Dimpled (Raised Nub) Tiles: Specify for drive pulleys. Raised ceramic nubs, typically 1 to 2 mm [0.04 to 0.08 in] high, indent the bottom cover under tension, maintaining friction in wet or contaminated conditions. Specify only where the friction gain justifies the increased bottom cover wear.
  • Smooth (Flat) Tiles: Specify for non-drive pulleys (tail, take-up and bend). The belt micro-slips as it bends around non-drive pulleys, so dimpled tiles in these positions accelerate bottom cover wear. Smooth tiles protect the shell while permitting micro-slip.

4.5 Lagging Selection, Cost and Maintenance

Rules of Thumb:

  • Standard plant drives (low to medium tension): hot vulcanised diamond grooved rubber.
  • High-tension, wet or slipping drives: hot vulcanised dimpled rubber-backed ceramic. The higher friction factor reduces slip and permits a reduction in take-up mass.
  • High-wear non-drive pulleys (tails and bends in abrasive ore): hot vulcanised smooth ceramic or cast polyurethane.
  • Emergency field repair: cold vulcanised rubber or cold bonded ceramic. Replace with a hot vulcanised spare at the next major shutdown.

5. Tension Transfer and Lagging Shear Mechanics

Lagging shear failure and macroscopic slip are governed by interfacial mechanics. Motor torque is transferred from the steel shell to the belt through surface friction and shear strain in the lagging.

5.1 The T1 to T2 Transition (Arc of Contact)

A driven belt enters the pulley at the tight side tension (T1) and exits at the slack side tension (T2). Tension does not drop uniformly around the wrap. The arc of contact has two zones:

  • Inactive (Static) Angle: Over the first portion of the wrap, the belt and lagging surface travel at the same speed. There is no relative movement and no tension change. The belt remains at T1.
  • Active (Creep) Angle: Approaching the exit, the belt contracts elastically to the lower T2 tension and moves slightly slower than the pulley surface. This continuous micro-slip zone is the only part of the wrap where tension falls, decreasing exponentially from T1 to T2.

5.2 Shear Stress and Strain in Pulley Lagging

The steel shell drives the base of the lagging forward while the belt resists at the surface, generating shear stress through the lagging thickness. Thin lagging accommodates belt micro-slip through high shear strain, concentrating shear stress at the bond line and promoting debonding. Thicker lagging distributes the same micro-slip over a greater thickness, reducing shear strain and peak shear stress at the bond line.

5.3 Effect of Lagging Type on Shear and Performance Limits

Lagging limits are governed by material compliance. High-compliance elastomers absorb shear internally but are susceptible to cohesive tearing under peak loads. Low-compliance materials transfer shear directly to the bond line, causing delamination or brittle fracture under shell flexure.

6. Pulley Structural Design and Shaft Sizing

Pulleys are structural components subject to cyclic rotating bending, in which the resultant of T1 and T2 generates multi-axial stresses. Inadequate fatigue assessment of the shaft, locking assemblies and end-discs can cause fatigue fracture.

6.1 Shaft Design Standards, Fatigue and Stress Concentrations

A conveyor pulley shaft is subject to two simultaneous loads:

  • Torsion: Drive torque (drive pulleys only).
  • Reversing Bending: Radial load from belt tension, producing a completely reversed bending cycle as the shaft rotates under a fixed load direction.

Designing solely for static yield permits fatigue cracks to initiate at stress concentrations such as shoulders, keyways and locking assembly edges. Assess shaft fatigue against the material endurance limit to AS 1403 or DIN 743.

Higher UTS raises the endurance limit but also increases notch sensitivity. Specify generous fillet radii at shaft shoulders.

6.2 Shaft Deflection Limits and Bearing Kinematics

Compliance with fatigue criteria does not limit elastic shaft deflection. Excessive deflection causes micro-slip at the locking assemblies and binding of bearings and seals.

Shaft bending creates a slope through the hub. A steep slope pries the locking assembly, causing micro-slip and fretting corrosion (red oxide powder at the hub interface). The locking assembly can eventually lose grip, allowing the shaft to rotate within the hub. Limit shaft slope at the hub to 0.0015 rad, reduced to 0.0010 rad for steel cord belts.

Bearing self-alignment capacity typically exceeds the misalignment tolerance of rigid labyrinth seals. Limit the angular deflection of the shaft at the bearing journal to 5 to 10 arc-minutes (0.08 to 0.17 deg) to preserve seal integrity.

6.3 Keyless Locking Assemblies

Specify keyless locking assemblies on high-tension pulleys to remove the keyway stress concentration that initiates fatigue cracks in keyed hub connections under cyclic bending. Locking assemblies consist of single or double tapered inner and outer steel rings. Tightening the high-tensile bolts wedges the rings together, expanding them into the hub and contracting them onto the shaft. The resulting uniform radial pressure locks the hub to the shaft by friction.

Locking assemblies differ from shrink discs, which clamp a hollow hub onto a shaft externally.

Do not select locking assemblies on torque alone. The cyclic bending moment from belt tension frequently exceeds drive torque, and non-drive pulleys carry bending alone. Excessive bending overcomes the clamping force on the tension side of the shaft, opening a radial gap that initiates slip, fretting corrosion and loss of interference fit.

Combine the applied torque and bending moment into a single equivalent torque using the manufacturer's method. Select an assembly whose rated torque, after the required service factor, equals or exceeds this equivalent torque. Each assembly also limits the proportion of rated torque that bending moment alone can consume. Exceeding this limit can gap or yield the tapered rings on the tension side.

Standard duty assemblies (single-taper or light-duty double-taper) are typically limited to a bending moment of 30% to 40% of rated torque. Heavy-duty assemblies with longer, shallower tapers and heavier clamping rings typically permit up to 60%. Confirm limits with the manufacturer.

6.5 Shell Material Selection and Minimum Thickness

The shell carries radial pressure, bending stress and compressive hoop stress from belt tension. Specify material grade and wall thickness to avoid shell buckling and fatigue cracking.

The shell is typically rolled and seam-welded from plate steel. Common grades include:

  • AS/NZS 3678-250 (Australia): Structural plate, minimum yield 250 MPa [36 ksi]. Specify AS/NZS 3678-350 (minimum yield 350 MPa [51 ksi]) for heavy-duty applications.
  • AS 1548 PT460 (formerly 7-460) (Australia): Pressure vessel plate, minimum yield 305 MPa [44 ksi] at 16 mm [0.63 in] thickness or less, reducing with thickness, with controlled chemistry and good weldability.
  • S355J2 (Europe, EN 10025-2): Structural steel, minimum yield 355 MPa [51 ksi].
  • ASTM A36 (North America): Structural steel, minimum yield 250 MPa [36 ksi]. Specify ASTM A572 Grade 50 (minimum yield 345 MPa [50 ksi]) for heavy-duty applications.

Determine minimum shell wall thickness from the combined radial pressure, hoop stress and shell bending stress.

Wide pulleys (face width greater than 1,800 mm [71 in]) deflect significantly at mid-span under distributed radial load. Do not use internal welded shell stiffener rings. Internal welds are difficult to access, preclude automated welding and make consistent weld quality and post-weld non-destructive testing (NDT) impractical.

Stiffener-to-shell fillet welds also introduce stress concentrations at the location of peak shell bending stress, reducing fatigue life. Instead, increase shell thickness or reduce the span between end-discs.

6.6 Weld Quality and Inspection

Weld defects are the primary initiation sites for fatigue cracks in pulleys. Perform all structural welds to qualified Welding Procedure Specifications (WPS) under the applicable standard:

  • AS/NZS 1554.5 (Australia/New Zealand): Welding of Steel Structures Subject to High Levels of Fatigue Loading. Specify for conveyor pulley fabrication. Do not use AS/NZS 1554.1 Category SP for end-disc and shell welds. Its weld quality requirements do not address the completely reversed bending of these welds. AS/NZS 1554.5 specifies stricter weld profile control, imperfection acceptance criteria and inspection requirements for cyclic loading.
  • EN ISO 5817 (Europe): Quality Levels for Imperfections in Fusion-Welded Joints. Specify quality level B (stringent) or C (intermediate) according to pulley duty.
  • AWS D1.1 (North America): Structural Welding Code, Steel. Apply the cyclically loaded connection provisions.

Specify full penetration welds for all circumferential end-disc to shell joints. The unfused root of a partial penetration weld acts as a crack-like defect perpendicular to the principal bending stress. Fatigue cracks initiate at the root, where surface NDT cannot detect them, and fatigue life is substantially lower than for a full penetration weld.

Specify post-weld heat treatment (PWHT) for heavy-duty pulleys and pulleys subject to high cyclic loading. Welding residual tensile stress can approach the yield strength of the parent material, adding a mean stress to the cyclic bending stress and accelerating fatigue crack initiation.

Specify the following minimum NDT regime for critical structural welds:

  • Shell Longitudinal Seam: 100% Ultrasonic Testing (UT) or Radiographic Testing (RT) to verify full penetration and absence of internal defects.
  • End-Disc to Shell Welds: 100% Magnetic Particle Testing (MT) of the external surface to detect surface-breaking cracks. For heavy-duty and steel cord belt pulleys, add UT of the weld root to detect lack of fusion.
  • Hub to Disc Welds (welded hub designs): 100% MT.

Re-inspect weld repairs to the original standard. Retain NDT records and weld maps in the pulley's permanent quality record.

6.8 Fixed and Float (Free) Bearing Arrangements

The pulley shaft and the conveyor structure expand at different rates. For 3 m [10 ft] bearing centres and a 30 deg C [54 deg F] temperature differential, axial shaft growth is approximately 1 mm [0.04 in]. Axially locking both bearings restricts this differential expansion, generating internal thrust loads that can cause bearing failure.

Install one fixed bearing and one float bearing per conveyor pulley.

  • Fixed bearing: The outer ring is axially locked in the housing by locating rings on both sides. This anchors the pulley axially and reacts thrust loads from belt tracking.
  • Float bearing: The locating rings are omitted. The outer ring slides in the housing to accommodate shaft expansion. A toroidal roller bearing accommodates the same displacement internally with its outer ring located.

On driven pulleys, locate the fixed bearing on the drive side (gearbox or motor end) and the float bearing on the non-drive side. This prevents shaft expansion from displacing the low-speed coupling and damaging drivetrain components.
