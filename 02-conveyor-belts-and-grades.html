1. Conveyor Belts and Grades

A conveyor belt comprises a carcass, which carries the operating tensions, absorbs impact and supports the load, and rubber or polyvinyl chloride (PVC) covers, which protect the carcass from abrasion, impact, heat, oil and chemical damage. Specify the carcass first for tension and load support, then select the cover for environmental and wear resistance.

2. Belt Constructions

3.1 Warp-to-Weft Strength Ratio

Warp (longitudinal) yarns carry operating tension and weft (transverse) yarns provide lateral stability, impact distribution and fastener retention. Weft strength is typically 10% to 25% of warp strength.

A lump striking the belt at 90 deg is absorbed primarily by the weft yarns. Insufficient weft strength causes impact breakthrough and longitudinal splitting between the warp cords. Standard catalogue weft strengths suit general service. For heavy, sharp lumps under high-impact loading or for belts with mechanical fasteners under high tension, specify a high-weft or reinforced-weft construction.

4. Belt Carcass Selection

Carcass selection balances flexibility against rigidity. The belt requires sufficient tensile strength to carry the operating tension, adequate transverse rigidity to support material across the idler junctions, sufficient transverse flexibility to conform to the trough and adequate longitudinal flexibility to wrap the pulleys.

Evaluate five criteria to select the carcass:

  • Belt Width: Dictated by capacity and lump size.
  • Maximum Operating Tension (T_max): The highest steady-state tension in the system.
  • Peak Tensions: Transient tensions during starting (acceleration) and stopping (braking).
  • Load Support (Minimum Plies/Thickness): The minimum carcass stiffness required to support the load across the idler junctions without creasing.
  • Troughability (Maximum Plies/Thickness): The maximum carcass stiffness allowed before the belt becomes too rigid to conform to the trough profile.

Multiply a tension in kN/m by 5.71 to obtain PIW. This conversion changes only the units and applies no safety factor. Because 1 kN/m equals 1 N/mm, the same factor converts N/mm to lbf/in. Note: With a 10:1 safety factor, the formula reduces to T_s = S_u / 1.75, since 10 / 5.71 = 1.7513. The 1.75 constant is valid only for a 10:1 safety factor. For steel cord belting at 6.7:1, the constant becomes 6.7 / 5.71 = 1.173. Recalculate the constant for any other safety factor.

Engineering Takeaway: A constant derived for one safety factor misstates the safe working tension at any other. Applying the steel cord constant of 1.173 to a fabric belt rated at 10:1 overstates its safe working tension by approximately 49%.

4.2 Starting and Braking Tensions

Evaluate acceleration and braking tension against the allowable peak tension of the belt.

  • Standard Conditions: Limit peak tension on starting or braking to 140% of the allowable steady-state working tension.
  • Severe Conditions: For short cycle times, frequent loaded Direct-On-Line (DOL) starts or heavy impact, reduce the rated vulcanised working tension by 15%. For mechanical fasteners, the reduction applies to the already-reduced fastened rating. Limit peak tension to 150% of this reduced rating.
  • Extreme Conditions: For poor maintenance, high-temperature materials or chemically aggressive environments, reduce the rated vulcanised working tension by 30%, on the same basis for mechanical fasteners. Limit peak tension to 160% of this reduced rating.

4.3 Mechanical Fasteners versus Vulcanised Splices

Standard practice specifies hot or cold vulcanised splices for plain weave plied belting. A stepped splice in an n-ply belt has a theoretical efficiency of (n - 1) / n of the belt strength, e.g. 67% for 3-ply and 75% for 4-ply. Single-ply straight-warp or aramid belts require specialised finger splicing. Standard stepped splices on these materials can reduce splice efficiency to below 50%.

Where mechanical fasteners (hinged clips or solid plate) are used, derate the allowable working tension for fastener stress concentration, typically by 20% to 25% on standard EP fabric relative to a vulcanised splice. Solid Woven (PVC) belts offer superior fastener retention owing to their interlocking weave.

4.4 Troughability and Load Support Mechanics

Transverse rigidity sets upper and lower limits on the ply count for a given belt width.

  • Troughability limit (excessive transverse rigidity): Excessive ply counts for a given belt width prevent the carcass from conforming to the trough profile. During empty operation, the belt spans the wing idlers without centre roll contact, resulting in erratic tracking and potential edge damage.
  • Load support limit (insufficient transverse rigidity): Insufficient ply counts allow high-density material to force the carcass into the idler junctions. This induces longitudinal creasing, progressive carcass fatigue and splitting.

4.5 Carcass Selection Procedure

Calculate the required unit working tension:

Engineering Takeaway: Required unit working tension is the primary input to the carcass rating. Widening the belt for capacity lowers the unit working tension but can exceed the load-support limit for the resulting lower ply count.

Then proceed through the remaining checks:

  1. Determine the splicing method. Where mechanical fasteners are used, divide unit working tension by 0.75 before comparing it with the vulcanised rating, or compare unit working tension directly with the fastened rating.
  2. Select a carcass with an allowable working tension equal to or greater than unit working tension.
  3. Check troughability. Confirm the belt width equals or exceeds the minimum width required to trough the selected ply count.
  4. Check load support. Confirm the belt width does not exceed the maximum width for the bulk density of the material.
  5. Check pulley diameters. Confirm the installed pulley diameters meet the minimum diameters for the selected carcass.

5. General Properties of Belt Covers and Grades

Compounding modifies baseline polymer properties. The cover grade defines the minimum tensile, elongation and abrasion properties of the compound and its resistance to heat, oil and fire.

5.1 Cover Performance Standards and Testing

Belt cover physical properties are verified by laboratory testing.

  • Tensile Strength: Stress at rupture of the cover rubber, in MPa. Minimum values range from 15 to 25 MPa [2,200 to 3,600 psi] for general service grades (AS 1332 / ISO 10247 / DIN 22102). Higher tensile strength correlates with improved cut and gouge resistance.
  • Elongation at Break: Strain at rupture, typically 300% to 450% for standard NR/SBR grades, and an indicator of impact absorption without cracking. Low elongation (below 300%) indicates a hard, brittle compound that spalls and fractures under severe lump impact.
  • Abrasion Resistance (Volume Loss): Measured per ISO 4649 (formerly DIN 53516) by pressing a sample against a rotating abrasive drum and measuring volume lost in mm3. This is a primary comparative metric for cover selection. A lower volume loss correlates with higher abrasion resistance. Legacy DIN 53516 values are comparable and may be read directly against ISO 4649 thresholds.

Volume loss below 100 mm3 indicates high abrasion resistance. Specialty compounds (heat, oil or fire resistant) typically range from 150 to 250 mm3, as chemical or fire performance takes precedence over wear life. Do not select heat- or oil-resistant covers on abrasion performance alone.

The DIN grades W, X and Y do not form a linear hierarchy. Grade Y (150 mm3 maximum) functions as the standard baseline. Grade W (90 mm3 maximum) is formulated for sliding-wear resistance and exhibits lower performance under heavy impact or gouging. Grade X (120 mm3 maximum) provides above-baseline abrasion resistance alongside enhanced cut, impact and gouge resistance.

Cover Hardness (Shore A Durometer) is typically 55 to 65 Shore A for general service grades. Harder compounds (65 to 75 Shore A) resist abrasive cutting but chip more readily under impact. Softer compounds (50 to 58 Shore A) absorb impact better but wear faster under sliding abrasion. Retain the manufacturer's standard hardness for the selected grade unless the duty requires otherwise.

5.2 Cover-to-Carcass Adhesion

Cover delamination causes premature carcass failure irrespective of initial cover thickness.

Adhesion values near the specified minimum on a test certificate can indicate substandard calendering or contaminated resorcinol-formaldehyde-latex (RFL) treatment. Specify adhesion 25% to 50% above the minimum values. Low field adhesion manifests as blistering or delamination, especially in hot, humid or chemically aggressive environments. For steel cord belts, the equivalent metric is cord pull-out strength.

5.3 Static Conductivity and Anti-Static Requirements

The electrical resistance of a belt dictates whether it can safely dissipate static charges built up by friction against idlers and pulleys. Static discharge in environments containing combustible bulk materials constitutes an explosion hazard.

AS 1334.9 / ISO 284 measures surface resistance between electrodes on each cover surface. For standard anti-static service, surface resistance must be less than 300 MOhm (the general requirement for above-ground bulk handling). For FRAS (underground or hazardous) service, the belt must meet regional fire and static standards (AS 4606, EN 14973, Mine Safety and Health Administration (MSHA) Part 14). AS 4606 and EN 14973 mandate low surface resistance, gallery fire tests for propagation resistance and drum friction tests, which simulate a stalled belt on a running drive pulley. MSHA Part 14 mandates flame tests only.

Under AS 4606, resistance must be verified independently on both top and bottom covers. This is particularly relevant for Grade S belts, where the two covers may use different rubber formulations (for example a thicker, abrasion-resistant top cover and a thinner, higher-friction bottom cover). Confirm that both surfaces are independently certified.

Standard NR/SBR compounds are typically semi-conductive owing to their carbon black filler. Oil-resistant NBR and heat-resistant EPDM compounds are not inherently anti-static. The DIN Grade K definition includes anti-static performance to ISO 284. Where NBR or EPDM is specified for a combustible-dust environment, add an explicit anti-static requirement (surface resistance less than 300 MOhm per AS 1334.9 / ISO 284 / DIN 22104) to the purchase specification and confirm it on the test certificate.

Heat accelerates degradation of the cover-to-carcass bond. Above a sustained 120 deg C [248 deg F], standard cover-to-carcass bonding systems lose adhesion. For continuous operation above 150 deg C [302 deg F], confirm the heat-stable adhesion system with the manufacturer, typically modified phenolic or resorcinol-based bonding agents between the EPDM cover and polyester carcass.

Material temperature differs from belt cover temperature. Peak thermal exposure occurs transiently at the loading zone, and the belt cover temperature is lower than the peak material temperature by an amount that depends on cycle time, bed depth and contact duration. A belt carrying 200 deg C [392 deg F] clinker therefore does not necessarily require a 200 deg C [392 deg F] rated compound if the cycle time is long and the material bed thin.

5.6 Oil and Chemical Resistant Cover Grades

Oil swells natural rubber covers, causing blistering and cover separation from the carcass. Terpenes (found in woodchips) and vegetable and animal oils (grain, fertiliser) require moderate oil resistance (MOR), whereas mineral oils (treated coal, petroleum coke) require nitrile-based super oil resistance (SOR).

Two textile constructions are commonly certified to AS 4606 for Australian underground coal mining. Solid Woven PVC is a single-matrix PVC-impregnated interlocked carcass with PVC or thin rubber covers. It resists delamination, retains mechanical fasteners and is common practice for development conveyors that are frequently cut, shortened and re-fastened. Grade S plied rubber is a standard multi-ply EP/PN carcass with Grade S FRAS covers. It provides higher abrasion resistance and drive friction than PVC and accepts vulcanised splices for higher joint strength. Selection depends on how frequently the conveyor length changes. For longwall gate roads and continuous miner development panels, where the belt is cut and rejoined repeatedly, specify Solid Woven PVC with mechanical fasteners. For a fixed-length main trunk or drift conveyor spliced once and operated for years, specify Grade S plied rubber for its wear life, drive friction and splice efficiency.

PVC covers have a lower coefficient of friction than rubber, so a Solid Woven PVC belt on a standard rubber-lagged drive pulley may slip under high tension or in wet conditions where Grade S would not. Where Solid Woven PVC is specified for a high-tension underground trunk conveyor, specify ceramic drive pulley lagging.

DIN Grade K and AS Grade S are comparable fire-resistant compound designations with different minimum specification limits. At the specification limits, DIN K is the more demanding grade, with a minimum tensile strength of 20 MPa [2,900 psi] versus 14 MPa [2,000 psi], minimum elongation at break of 400% versus 300% and maximum abrasion volume loss of 200 mm3 versus 250 mm3. Compare certified test values rather than grade letters when selecting between them.

Specification must distinguish between compound formulation and system-level performance. DIN Grade K and AS Grade S are rubber compound specifications defining the cover chemistry. A fire-rated compound alone does not make a fire-rated belt. The complete construction (compound, carcass, covers and splicing method) must be tested and certified as a system. A DIN K-compliant compound may fail system-level gallery fire tests (e.g. EN 14973) if the internal carcass allows flame propagation. System-level certification (AS 4606, EN 14973 or MSHA Part 14) is mandatory for underground and enclosed hazardous service.

AS 4606 (Australia), EN 14973 (Europe) and MSHA Part 14 (USA) are not mutually recognised. A belt certified to EN 14973 is not accepted under AS 4606 without explicit approval from the relevant state mines inspectorate. A belt operating in multiple jurisdictions must be independently tested and certified to each applicable standard, or carry written regulatory exemptions from each authority.

The term "semi-FRAS" has no formal standard definition. It typically refers to a belt that passes the ISO 340 laboratory flame test but has not been gallery-fire-tested. ISO 340 is a small-scale, low-energy test in still air, whereas an underground mine fire involves forced ventilation airflow of 1.0 to 3.0 m/s [200 to 590 fpm] through a confined tunnel supplying oxygen to the flame front. Passing ISO 340 therefore does not demonstrate resistance to flame propagation along a full belt. ISO 340-rated belts must not be substituted for AS 4606, EN 14973 or MSHA Part 14 certified belts in underground or enclosed hazardous environments. State the required test certification and regulatory jurisdiction in the procurement specification.

DIN K compounds are produced on CR or SBR bases.

CR-based compounds use chlorine atoms in the backbone to achieve inherent self-extinguishing properties, low flame propagation and lower abrasion volume loss than SBR-based DIN K (volume loss typically 130 to 160 mm3). The service temperature range is limited to minus 10 to 110 deg C [14 to 230 deg F].

SBR-based compounds achieve DIN K fire retardancy through inorganic fillers (alumina trihydrate, magnesium hydroxide or antimony trioxide) synergised with halogenated organic compounds. These fillers reduce tensile strength, elongation and abrasion resistance, and typically leave the compound near the DIN limits (abrasion 180 to 200 mm3). Advantages are a wider cold-weather range (minus 30 to 70 deg C [minus 22 to 158 deg F]) and lower cost. The limitation is lower fire performance.

Where the belt requires EN 14973 underground FRAS certification, specify a CR-based DIN K compound. For above-ground ISO 340 fire retardancy only, an SBR-based DIN K compound is acceptable at lower cost, provided anti-static performance is confirmed on the test certificate.

The DIN 22102 maximum abrasion volume loss for Grade K (200 mm3) is 33% higher than Grade Y (150 mm3) and 122% higher than Grade W (90 mm3). At these limits, a DIN K top cover handling abrasive iron ore fines wears approximately 33% faster than an equivalent-thickness DIN Y cover. Where a fire-rated belt is required in a heavily abrasive application, increase top cover thickness by approximately one third compared with a Grade Y belt to achieve equivalent service life.

Above-ground coal handling facilities frequently specify DIN K with ISO 340 even where no underground FRAS regulation applies. The cost premium over DIN Y is typically 10% to 20% per metre (indicative). Specify DIN K with ISO 340 as the minimum for any enclosed gallery handling combustible bulk material.

6. Cover Gauges and Qualities

The top cover resists impact, abrasion, cutting and gouging from the load. The bottom (pulley-side) cover resists abrasion and friction from idlers and drive pulleys.

Disproportionate cover wear relative to carcass fatigue indicates insufficient cover thickness or suboptimal grade selection. Conversely, carcass failure with substantial cover remaining indicates excessive cover thickness or an under-rated carcass. Match cover wear life to carcass fatigue life.

6.1 Considerations for Selection

Select belt grade and cover thickness against the following factors:

  • Type and Abrasiveness of Material: Fine, sharp materials (glass cullet, iron ore fines) require high abrasion resistance and limited impact protection, whereas large lumps (ROM rock, primary crushed ore) require thick, gouge-resistant covers.
  • Loading Kinematics: Material trajectory and free-fall height directly dictate the required top cover thickness to mitigate impact penetration.
  • Cycle Frequency: Shorter conveyors pass each belt section through the loading zone more frequently, necessitating thicker covers to achieve equivalent wear life.
  • Operating Environment: Fire resistance, anti-static, oil resistance and heat resistance requirements.
  • Availability and Cost: Non-standard cover thicknesses can increase lead times and minimum order quantities.

6.2 Cycle Time Calculation

Cycle time (T_c), the period of one complete belt revolution, is a primary input to cover thickness selection.

Engineering Takeaway: Each belt section passes the loading zone once per cycle. Halving the cycle time doubles the rate of impact and abrasion exposure, so short conveyors require thicker covers for the same material.

Rules of Thumb:

  • Less than 30 seconds: High-frequency cycle. The belt is subject to near-continuous impact loading and requires a thicker cover for the specified lump size.
  • 30 to 180 seconds: Standard plant conveyor.
  • Greater than 180 seconds: Low-frequency cycle (e.g. overland conveyors). Cover thickness can be reduced, lowering belt mass and power consumption.

6.3 Pulley Side (Bottom) Cover Selection

The bottom cover is not exposed to impact or abrasion from the conveyed material. It is subject to continuous friction against the pulley lagging and idler rolls.

Rule of Thumb: Specify a pulley-side cover of one quarter to one third of the top cover thickness.

Specify the following minimum pulley-side cover thicknesses:

  • Light duty (sand, grain, woodchips): 1.5 mm [1/16 in].
  • Medium duty (crushed coal, limestone): 3.0 mm [1/8 in].
  • Heavy duty (ROM ores, high tension): 5.0 to 6.0 mm [3/16 to 1/4 in].

Where the belt is to be turned over during its service life to use both covers, specify equal top and bottom cover thickness. Where heavily abrasive spillage reaches the return run and is trapped between the belt and the pulleys, increase the bottom cover thickness to protect the carcass.

6.5 Energy Saving (Low Rolling Resistance) Covers

On long horizontal conveyors, bottom cover indentation rolling resistance constitutes a major portion of total system power consumption. Due to viscoelastic hysteresis, rubber deformation over the idlers absorbs kinetic energy, dissipating it as heat.

Indentation resistance depends strongly on ambient temperature. Below freezing, polymer chain mobility reduces. At minus 30 deg C [minus 22 deg F], standard compounds stiffen, increasing indentation resistance and power consumption.

A low rolling resistance (LRR) bottom cover on a horizontal conveyor longer than 1,000 m [3,300 ft] can reduce indentation rolling resistance by 10% to 20%. The resulting reduction in T_max can permit a lower-rated carcass (e.g. EP 800/4 in place of EP 1000/4). Specify cold-weather LRR compounds for sub-zero operation. These use plasticisers to maintain polymer flexibility down to minus 40 deg C [minus 40 deg F].

LRR compounds typically have lower abrasion resistance and tear strength than standard grades. They are rarely justified on short plant conveyors, where lift and secondary resistances dominate the tension calculation.

7. Solid Woven Belting

Solid woven belting replaces discrete fabric plies bonded with rubber skim coats with a single interlocked weave of synthetic fibres impregnated with PVC. With no individual plies, it cannot delaminate, which makes it the common construction for underground coal mining, potash mining and other enclosed, high-hazard environments where fire resistance and anti-static performance are regulated.

7.1 Belt Construction

Solid Woven belting consists of three main components. The textile carcass is typically woven with polyester or nylon load-bearing warp (longitudinal) fibres and nylon or nylon/cotton weft (transverse) fibres, with synthetic binder yarns locking the warp and weft together. High-tenacity continuous filament yarns provide the tension rating, while the interlocking cotton/nylon weft provides impact resistance and a dense matrix that retains mechanical fasteners.

PVC impregnation follows weaving. The textile passes through a liquid PVC plastisol bath under tension, saturating the matrix and displacing moisture and air. The PVC is formulated with plasticisers, ultraviolet (UV) stabilisers and fire retardants and renders the carcass resistant to moisture, acidic mine water, bacteria and oils.

Cover materials complete the construction. Standard PVC covers up to 3 mm [1/8 in] per side can be applied integrally during manufacture. Because PVC has a lower coefficient of friction than rubber, PVC-covered belts can slip on drive pulleys without ceramic lagging and are prone to material rollback on inclines steeper than 12 to 14 deg. Where higher friction or greater abrasion resistance is needed, rubber (nitrile/SBR) covers can be bonded to the PVC carcass, typically to a maximum of 6 mm [1/4 in] top and 2 mm [5/64 in] bottom.

7.2 Operating Temperature Range

Thermoplastic-based Solid Woven belts exhibit higher sensitivity to thermal extremes compared to vulcanised elastomeric belts. Standard PVC compounds experience structural degradation at 70 to 80 deg C [158 to 176 deg F]. Exceeding this range induces matrix softening, loss of transverse stiffness, collapse into the idler junctions and degraded mechanical fastener retention. Limit continuous service to 70 deg C [158 deg F] and short-term peaks to 90 deg C [194 deg F]. Do not use PVC-covered Solid Woven belts for hot materials such as clinker or hot sinter.

Standard PVC compounds become stiff and brittle at approximately minus 15 deg C [5 deg F]. Specify cold-weather plastisol formulations for service down to minus 40 deg C [minus 40 deg F] to resist cracking at the pulleys, and state the minimum operating temperature in the purchase specification.

8. Steel Cord Belting

Steel cord belting is the highest-tension standard construction for bulk handling. It is used on conveyors where high belt tension, limited take-up travel or the requirement for a high elastic modulus makes a fabric multi-ply belt unsuitable.

Steel cord belts carry a higher initial cost than fabric belting but provide high splice efficiency, smaller pulleys relative to tension rating and reduced take-up travel owing to low elongation.

8.1 Construction and Designations

Steel cord belts consist of a single layer of evenly spaced, zinc-galvanised high-tensile steel wire ropes (cords) embedded in a bonding rubber matrix and protected by top and bottom rubber covers. The ST prefix is followed by the ultimate breaking strength in kN/m (e.g. ST 2000 denotes 2,000 kN/m [11,400 PIW]).

With no transverse fabric plies to arrest a sharp lump penetrating the cover, ST belts require a thicker top cover than fabric belts. Specify a top cover of at least 1.5 times the cord diameter.

10. Belt Manufacturing Tolerances

Conveyor belting is manufactured to specified dimensional tolerances. Distinguish tracking deviations caused by structural misalignment from those caused by out-of-specification belt geometry.

10.1 Width Tolerance

AS 1332, ISO 251 and DIN 22102 specify consistent width tolerances.

  • Belts up to 500 mm [20 in] wide: The allowable tolerance is +/- 5.0 mm [3/16 in].
  • Belts over 500 mm [20 in] wide: The allowable tolerance is +/- 1.0% of the nominal specified width.

10.2 Total Thickness Tolerance

AS 1332, ISO 14890 and DIN 22102 specify the following total belt thickness (carcass and covers) tolerances.

  • Belts with a total thickness of 10 mm [3/8 in] or less: The tolerance is +/- 1.0 mm [0.04 in].
  • Belts thicker than 10 mm [3/8 in]: The tolerance is +/- 10% of the total specified thickness.

10.3 Cover Thickness and Zero Negative Specifications

Standard tolerances permit positive and negative cover thickness deviation. AS 1332, ISO 14890 and DIN 22102 permit +1.0 mm [0.04 in] to minus 0.2 mm [0.008 in] for covers up to 4.0 mm [5/32 in] thick and generally +/- 5.0% for thicker covers.

Cover thickness is measured from the outer surface to the first reinforcing ply or cord layer and therefore includes the skim coat bonding the cover to the carcass. A manufacturer applying the full negative tolerance (e.g. minus 5.0%) and counting the skim coat as cover delivers less wear rubber than specified, reducing wear life in proportion.

For heavily abrasive applications such as iron ore, crushed granite or glass cullet, specify a "Zero Negative Cover Tolerance" during procurement. This clause overrides standard ISO/DIN average thickness and localised deviation allowances. It contractually binds the manufacturer to a (+X, minus 0) tolerance at all measurable points across the belt width.

10.4 Straightness (Camber / Bow)

A conveyor belt must be manufactured straight along its length. A permanent lateral curve (camber or bow) induces persistent asymmetric tracking irrespective of structural alignment. The maximum permissible camber per ISO 14890 / DIN 22102 is 0.5% of the measured length. Over a 10 m [33 ft] sample laid flat, the belt edge must not deviate from a straight line by more than 50 mm [2 in].
