1. Fire Detection and Suppression Systems

Friction from slipping drive pulleys or seized idler bearings, combined with ventilation airflow and fuel loads, presents a high fire risk in enclosed bulk handling facilities.

Fire Resistant Anti-Static (FRAS) belting self-extinguishes upon heat source removal but does not prevent initial ignition or extinguish burning bulk materials.

2. Fire Prevention Controls

Prevention controls reduce ignition sources before detection and suppression systems are required.

Belt slip and lateral drift generate sustained frictional heat. Monitor belt slip by comparing drive pulley rotational speed (encoder/tachometer) with belt surface velocity (contact-wheel sensor). Set the slip trip at a velocity differential of typically 5% to 10%. Detect belt drift with mechanical drift switches.

Hot work such as welding, grinding, and cutting requires a Hot Work Permit. Permit conditions include:

  • Removal or protection of combustible material within 11 m [35 ft] of the work
  • A designated fire watch during the work and for at least 60 minutes after completion, per NFPA 51B
  • Local first-response suppression equipment
  • Post-work area inspection

3. Early Warning: CO and Smoke Detection

Smouldering releases Carbon Monoxide (CO) before visible smoke or flame. Mount electrochemical CO sensors along the conveyor and at transfer point exhaust paths to monitor baseline parts-per-million (ppm) levels. Space sensors to the applicable regulation or the site fire risk assessment. For US underground coal belt entries, Mine Safety and Health Administration (MSHA) regulation 30 CFR 75.1103-4 limits spacing to 300 m [1,000 ft], or 105 m [350 ft] where air velocity is less than 0.25 m/s [50 fpm], and requires a sensor within 30 m [100 ft] downwind of each drive, tail, transfer point and take-up.

30 CFR 75.351 sets the alert at 5 ppm and the alarm at 10 ppm above ambient for US belt air courses. A rising CO trend triggers a pre-alarm to halt the conveyor and isolate the smouldering material before ignition.

Point smoke detectors are unsuitable for high-velocity draughts. Aspirating Smoke Detectors (ASD) use a continuous polyvinyl chloride (PVC) pipe network along the gallery roof with engineered sampling holes. An aspirator draws air to a central laser detection chamber that detects sub-visible smoke particles.

4. Linear Heat Detection

Linear Heat Detection (LHD) locates elevated temperatures along the conveyor to enable targeted suppression. A twisted pair of spring-tensioned steel conductors, each insulated with a heat-sensitive polymer, is installed along the conveyor stringers. When the cable temperature reaches its rated threshold (e.g. 85 deg C [185 deg F]), the polymer melts, shorting the conductors. The fire panel measures loop resistance to determine the distance to the fault. Replace the activated section after each alarm. The cable is susceptible to mechanical damage.

Fibre-Optic Distributed Temperature Sensing (DTS) maps the continuous temperature profile along the conveyor using Raman backscatter. The control unit transmits a laser pulse along the fibre and derives temperature from the ratio of anti-Stokes to Stokes backscatter intensity. Spatial resolution is typically 1.0 m [3.3 ft], up to 2.0 m [6.6 ft] on long runs.

Configure DTS alarms in two stages:

  • Warning: 70 to 80 deg C [158 to 176 deg F], or a temperature rise of 5 to 10 deg C [9 to 18 deg F] per minute. Investigate the alarm location.
  • Trip: below the ignition temperature of the conveyed material, with margin, or at a higher rate of rise. Stop the conveyor or release suppression. For coal, set the trip at no more than 100 deg C [212 deg F], below the typical coal dust layer ignition temperature of 140 to 160 deg C [284 to 320 deg F].

5. Automated Deluge and Water Curtain Systems

High-volume water deluge is the primary suppression method for transfer towers, drive stations, and enclosed galleries. Wet-pipe systems are generally unsuitable due to freezing risks, thermal expansion, and accidental discharge hazards. Deluge systems use open nozzles on dry, unpressurised pipework downstream of the deluge valve. Water is isolated at the deluge valve in a protected zone. On fire confirmation, the fire detection and releasing panel opens the valve, discharging firewater simultaneously across the fire zone.

Water curtains mitigate chimney-effect fire propagation. Install wide-angle nozzles at typically 50 m [165 ft] intervals and at penetrations through blast walls and portals. Atomised water mist impedes radiant heat transmission and provides evaporative cooling.

Deluge systems discharge typically 3,000 to 5,000 litres per minute [800 to 1,300 gallons per minute], introducing structural loading risks from standing water. Provide redundant drainage on elevated conveyor structures protected by deluge. Use open-grid walkways. Grade solid floors to drains sized to evacuate 150% of the maximum design deluge flow rate, assuming 50% blockage by bulk material.

6. Gas and Foam Suppression

Use alternative suppression media where water introduces unacceptable hazards.

Specify high-expansion foam for underground tunnels where water-based suppression risks flooding, electrical short-circuits, and infrastructure damage. Foam concentrate is aerated to expansion ratios of 200:1 to 1,000:1. Generators fill the tunnel volume, displacing oxygen and cooling exposed surfaces with minimal liquid residue.

In fully enclosed transfer chutes, crushers, and grain silos, water applied to confined smouldering material risks steam-expansion events or product contamination. Use inert gas (nitrogen or carbon dioxide (CO2)) in these spaces. High-pressure cylinders discharge gas to reduce the oxygen (O2) concentration below the Limiting Oxygen Concentration (LOC) of the material, typically 12% to 15% O2 for carbonaceous materials. Set the design margin below the LOC to NFPA 69. Smouldering combustion persists at lower O2 concentrations than flaming combustion, so maintain the inert atmosphere until the material has cooled.

Fit inert gas systems with physical safety interlocks against asphyxiation. Inert gas systems must be locked out before personnel entry.

7. Integration with Conveyor Control Systems

Interface the fire detection system directly with the conveyor Programmable Logic Controller (PLC). Do not rely on standalone manual systems for enclosed or underground installations. Configure the following automatic actions on fire detection:

  • Emergency stop of the affected conveyor and upstream feeding conveyors.
  • Isolation of drive motors and electrical equipment within the fire zone.
  • Closure of affected ventilation fire dampers.
  • Activation of the applicable deluge or suppression zone through the fire detection and releasing panel.
  • Transmission of alarm signals to the emergency control point.
  • Lockout of belt restart controls against inadvertent re-energisation.

Reset a fire-tripped conveyor manually, only after physical inspection. Provide an uninterruptible power supply (UPS) for the fire detection and control system.

8. Manual First-Response Suppression Equipment

Manual suppression is a supplementary measure for incipient fires only. Beyond the incipient stage, evacuation takes precedence. Install portable dry powder (ABE) or CO2 extinguishers at maximum 30 m [100 ft] intervals along enclosed galleries, transfer points, and drive stations. Select extinguishers to suit the electrical and material hazards present, and locate them to AS 2444.

Provide fixed hose reels to AS 2441 giving full structural coverage of transfer towers, with a minimum flow rate of 0.33 litres per second [5.2 gallons per minute] for 19 mm [0.75 in] hose. Where a higher first-attack flow is required, provide hydrants to AS 2419.1, rated at 10 litres per second [160 gallons per minute] per outlet. Fixed monitor nozzles provide remote attack capability for stockpiles and exposed structures.

9. Emergency Evacuation and Personnel Egress

Provide at least two independent, unobstructed egress routes from enclosed conveyor installations, physically separated from the conveyor and combustible dust zones. Provide egress walkways with a minimum clear width of 750 mm [30 in] and clear headroom of 2,000 mm [79 in].

Provide battery-backed emergency escape lighting to AS/NZS 2293.1, which requires a minimum illuminance of 0.2 lux [0.02 foot-candle] at floor level on the escape path centreline, 1 lux [0.09 foot-candle] on stairs, and a minimum duration of 90 minutes after loss of normal supply. Specify a higher illuminance where the site fire risk assessment requires it.

Provide refuge chambers in underground drifts where the egress time exceeds the limit set by the applicable mining regulation or site fire risk assessment. Interface electronic personnel tracking with the emergency response plan for muster and accountability.

Coordinate full-flow deluge tests with plant operations to confirm drainage capacity and protect equipment. Record all maintenance in a fire system register with defined corrective actions for identified deficiencies.

11. Applicable Standards and Regulatory References

Applicable national and international standards include:

  • AS 1670.1: Fire Detection, Warning, Control and Intercom Systems
  • AS 1851: Routine Service of Fire Protection Systems and Equipment
  • AS 2118.3: Automatic Fire Sprinkler Systems, Part 3: Deluge Systems
  • AS 2419.1: Fire Hydrant Installations, Part 1: System Design, Installation and Commissioning
  • AS 2441: Installation of Fire Hose Reels
  • AS 2444: Portable Fire Extinguishers and Fire Blankets, Selection and Location
  • AS/NZS 2293.1: Emergency Lighting and Exit Signs for Buildings, Part 1: System Design, Installation and Operation
  • ISO 340: Conveyor Belts, Laboratory Scale Flammability Characteristics, Requirements and Test Method
  • NFPA 13: Standard for the Installation of Sprinkler Systems
  • NFPA 25: Standard for the Inspection, Testing, and Maintenance of Water-Based Fire Protection Systems
  • NFPA 51B: Standard for Fire Prevention During Welding, Cutting, and Other Hot Work
  • NFPA 69: Standard on Explosion Prevention Systems
  • NFPA 72: National Fire Alarm and Signaling Code
  • MSHA 30 CFR Part 75: Mandatory Safety Standards for Underground Coal Mines

Where standards conflict, apply the most stringent requirement. Base the design on a site-specific fire risk assessment by a competent fire safety engineer.
