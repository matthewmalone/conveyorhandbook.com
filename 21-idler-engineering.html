1. Idler Engineering

Idler diameter, bearing selection, and spacing determine a large share of conveyor main resistance and drive power, and directly affect belt service life and system availability.

2. Idler Load Ratings

In North America, idlers are specified by Conveyor Equipment Manufacturers Association (CEMA) load class. Elsewhere, standards define roller geometry only, and the bearing, shaft, and shell are selected or engineered to suit the application load and speed.

2.1 The North American System

In the USA and Canada, CEMA defines prescriptive idler classes (B through F). CEMA 502 defines each class by load rating, roll diameter, and frame mounting dimensions.

Ratings are based on a minimum L10 bearing life (the life that 90% of a bearing population reaches or exceeds) at 500 revolutions per minute (RPM). The class letter denotes the load rating and the number denotes the roll diameter in inches (e.g. D6). Shaft and bearing sizes are set by the manufacturer to meet the rating. Typical shaft sizes are:

  • CEMA B (Light Duty): 16 mm [5/8 in] shafts. Used for light materials such as grain, woodchips, and fine sand. Belt widths up to 1,200 mm [48 in].
  • CEMA C (Medium Duty): 19 mm [3/4 in] shafts. Common for general plant use, crushed coal, and aggregate.
  • CEMA D (Heavy Duty): 25 to 32 mm [1 to 1.25 in] shafts. Used for heavy ores, large lump sizes, and high-capacity mining operations.
  • CEMA E (Extreme Duty): 32 to 35 mm [1.25 to 1.375 in] shafts. Used for Run-of-Mine (ROM) ore and primary crusher product.
  • CEMA F (Ultra-Heavy Duty): 38 to 45 mm [1.5 to 1.75 in] shafts and larger. Used for high-tonnage, high-speed systems.

CEMA tube diameters are:

  • 102 mm [4 in]: Used for slow, light-duty applications or return rolls on narrow belts.
  • 127 mm [5 in]: Common size for light to medium duty.
  • 152 mm [6 in]: Common size for heavy-duty CEMA D and E applications.
  • 178 mm [7 in]: Used for high-capacity, high-speed overland systems (5.0 m/s [1,000 fpm] and above).
  • 203 mm [8 in]: CEMA F rollers for high-capacity, high-speed applications.

2.2 Global Standards

Outside North America, ISO and DIN standards define roller geometry (roller lengths, diameters, and troughing angles) but do not prescribe load classes. Tube wall thickness, shaft diameter, and bearing series (e.g. 6204, 6306) are selected to achieve the required L10h life.

ISO/DIN and CEMA rollers are not interchangeable. ISO/DIN shells of 108, 133, and 159 mm [4.25, 5.25 and 6.25 in] are approximately 6 mm [0.25 in] larger than CEMA 102, 127 and 152 mm [4, 5 and 6 in] shells, and roll length and shaft end dimensions differ.

2.3 Australian Industry Practice (Engineered-to-Order)

Australia has no national standard equivalent to CEMA load classes. Historical internal standards from state utilities and major mining companies (e.g. State Electricity Commission of Victoria (SECV) coal standards, BHP and Rio Tinto iron ore standards) established standard drop-in lengths and shaft end flats for cross-supplier interchangeability. Current geometric dimensions follow these legacy standards, while internal designs remain bespoke and are engineered-to-order for site tonnage and speed.

For major Australian iron ore and coal operations, 152 mm [6 in] or 159 mm [6.25 in] rollers are typically the minimum for carrying idlers. 127 mm [5 in] and 133 mm [5.25 in] sizes are rarely specified for heavy-duty carry duty due to higher rotational speed, shorter bearing life, and lower shell wear allowance. Return roller diameters are typically matched to carrying rollers to standardise spares and extend shell wear life against the dirty belt side.

5. Roller Diameter, Bearing Life and Resistance

5.1 Bearing Life (L10h) and Roll Diameter

Idler service life is limited by bearing fatigue, seal failure and contamination, or shell wear. In dusty or wet service, seal performance typically governs. The L10h rating is the calculated operating time in hours by which 10% of a group of identical bearings will have failed by fatigue under a specific load and speed.

The target L10h for standard plant conveyors is typically 30,000 hours, whereas critical overland conveyors target 60,000 hours or more.

Bearing life L10h is inversely proportional to rotational speed and, for ball bearings, to the cube of bearing load. At constant belt speed, bearing and load, L10h is directly proportional to roll diameter.

5.2 Impact on Power Consumption and Rolling Resistance

Main resistance on long conveyors has three idler-related components:

  • Bearing and seal friction (rim drag): Bearing friction, grease churning, and seal drag. Contact seals and grease-filled labyrinths add drag. Low-drag labyrinth designs reduce this loss.
  • Indentation hysteresis: As the belt rolls over the idler, the bottom cover is compressed and rebounds, converting energy into heat. A larger diameter idler creates a broader, shallower indentation, reducing hysteresis loss.
  • Belt and material flexure: Belt sag between idlers flexes the belt and shears the bulk load, dissipating energy. Flexure resistance increases with idler spacing and sag ratio.

6.2 Seal Material Selection

Standard nitrile butadiene rubber (NBR) seals suit minus 20 to plus 100 deg C [minus 4 to plus 212 deg F]. For extreme cold (e.g. Arctic or high-altitude operations), specify low-temperature polyurethane or silicone compounds to resist embrittlement and cracking. For chemically aggressive environments (e.g. phosphate or sulphuric acid plants), specify fluoroelastomer (FKM) seals.

7.3 Rules of Thumb for Idler Geometry

Limit the gap between adjacent rolls in an inline idler set to 10 to 15 mm [3/8 to 5/8 in], with a maximum of 20 mm [3/4 in]. Under load, the belt cover is forced into the gap. If the gap is excessive, the belt is drawn into the pinch point between rotating rolls. This failure mode, junction failure (nipping), progressively degrades the bottom cover and initiates longitudinal carcass fatigue. Replace or rectify frames that exceed this limit.

Each time a belt passes over an idler, it bends at the roll junctions. If the carry-side and return-side junction lines align on the same longitudinal axis, the same belt fibres are repeatedly bent and reverse-bent, accelerating carcass fatigue. Select carry and return idler configurations whose junction lines do not align. A 3-roll carry idler places junctions at approximately one-third and two-thirds of the belt width. A 2-roll V-return places its single junction at the belt centreline. The stress lines do not coincide.

9. Roller Dimensional Tolerances

The roundness and diametrical accuracy of an idler roll affect conveyor vibration, noise, and belt scale accuracy.

TIR is the difference between the maximum and minimum radial readings over one full rotation. Elevated TIR produces harmonic belt flap and accelerates bearing failure.

TIR alone does not capture localised defects such as flat spots or weld seams. Maximum Indicated Slope (MIS) addresses this limitation and is defined as the maximum indicated runout per 6 deg of roller rotation.

A roller may exhibit acceptable TIR yet carry a high MIS, indicating a sharp geometric step on the surface. Specify both TIR and MIS for weigh idlers and high-speed overland conveyors.

10. Dynamic Balancing and Unbalance Limits

A roller can have acceptable TIR yet still vibrate if mass distribution within the rotating assembly is uneven. This condition is residual unbalance.

10.1 Sources of Residual Unbalance

Residual unbalance in an idler roller arises from:

  • Wall thickness variation. Non-uniform tube wall around the circumference offsets the mass centre.
  • Weld seam mass concentration. The internal weld bead on ERW tube creates a localised heavy spot unless removed.
  • End plate eccentricity. If the pressed or welded end plates are not concentric with the shell bore, the mass centre of each end plate is offset from the rotational axis.
  • Bearing housing asymmetry. Cast or machined bearing housings may have slight mass variations due to porosity, machining tolerances, or asymmetric ribbing.
  • Rubber lagging thickness variation. On impact idlers, uneven rubber sleeve thickness creates unbalance.

The shaft is stationary and does not contribute to unbalance.

10.2 The Effect of Unbalance

Residual unbalance generates rotating radial forces transmitted through the bearings into the idler frame and conveyor structure. The force increases with the square of rotational speed. Doubling speed from 300 to 600 RPM quadruples the force.

10.3 ISO 21940-11 Balance Grades

The international standard for specifying permissible residual unbalance is ISO 21940-11 (formerly ISO 1940-1).

Typical balance grades for conveyor idler rollers are:

  • G40: Standard conveyors below 500 RPM. Minimum grade commonly specified.
  • G16: Conveyors at 500 to 700 RPM and return idlers in noise-sensitive areas.
  • G6.3: Conveyors above 700 RPM, all weigh-deck idlers, and idlers within 50 m [165 ft] of noise-sensitive receptors. Requires two-plane balancing.

12. Idler Spacing and Belt Sag

Idler spacing and belt tension determine belt sag between supports. Limit carry-side sag to 1.5% to 2.0% of idler spacing and return-side sag to 3.0%.

12.1 Dynamic Material Jump (Trampoline Effect)

Where idler spacing is excessive for the applied tension, the belt sags between supports. The belt climbs out of this valley to pass over the next idler, where its path is abruptly flattened. At high belt speeds, this imparts a vertical velocity component to the load, launching material clear of the belt surface.

The resulting material jump increases cover abrasion, dust emission, spillage, and cyclic bearing loading.

12.2 Carry and Return Spacing

Carry spacing is set by loaded sag and idler load rating. Return spacing is wider because the return belt carries only its own mass.

13. Belt Tracking and Self-Aligning (Training) Idlers

Off-centre loading, wind, material build-up, and ground movement cause mistracking even on a well-aligned conveyor, resulting in spillage, structure contact and belt edge damage. Self-aligning idlers (tracking or training idlers) are installed at intervals to correct this.

13.1 How Self-Aligning Idlers Work

A self-aligning idler comprises a standard 3-roll troughing set (or 1-roll or 2-roll return set) mounted on a central pivot pin or slewing bearing.

Vertical guide rollers (sensor rollers) are mounted at the outer edges of the frame. When the belt wanders, its edge contacts a guide roller. Edge friction drags the guide roller forward, causing the frame to pivot (slew) on its central axis.

The skewed rolls steer the belt back toward the centre. Once the belt is centred, it no longer touches the guide rollers and the frame returns to its neutral position.

13.2 Rules of Thumb for Placement and Spacing

Self-aligning idlers supplement, but do not replace, conveyor alignment and square splices. Do not install training idlers within pulley transition zones.

Standard self-aligning idlers are directional. On a reversing conveyor, a standard trainer steers the belt further off-centre when running in reverse. Specify bi-directional trainers on reversing conveyors.

On the return side, the critical tracking point is where the empty belt enters the tail pulley. If it enters off-centre, it loads off-centre, amplifying mistracking along the carry run.

Rules of Thumb:

  • Place the first return training idler approximately 12 to 15 m [40 to 50 ft] upstream of the tail pulley.
  • Place additional return trainers every 30 to 60 m [100 to 200 ft] along the return run.

On the carry side, wandering is usually caused by off-centre loading. Centre the belt before it reaches the head pulley.

Rules of Thumb:

  • Place the first carry-side training idler approximately 12 to 15 m [40 to 50 ft] upstream of the head pulley.
  • Place additional carry-side trainers every 30 to 45 m [100 to 150 ft] along the carry run.

13.3 Fixed Side-Guide Rollers

Fixed (non-pivoting) vertical edge rollers do not steer the belt. They resist lateral belt force through edge contact, which can fold and delaminate the belt edge to expose the carcass. Fit them only as emergency edge protection, set clear of the belt edge in normal running.

16.4 Rules of Thumb for Alignment Practice

Rule of Thumb: Random alignment errors (some frames slightly left, some slightly right) are less damaging than systematic errors (every frame offset in the same direction). A random 2 mm [0.08 in] transverse error across 50 frames produces local belt wobble that training idlers can manage. A systematic 2 mm [0.08 in] transverse error across 50 frames produces cumulative lateral drift that can drive the belt off the structure. Keep residual errors random and self-cancelling.

Rule of Thumb: Re-check every frame on the affected span after stringer replacement, foundation re-grouting, gallery re-levelling, or underground roof bolt re-tensioning. Moving one end of a stringer by 3 mm [0.12 in] can skew every frame on that stringer.

Rule of Thumb: Foundation settlement, thermal cycling, and maintenance disturbance degrade commissioning alignment on permanent surface conveyors. Repeat a full alignment survey at least every 5 years on standard plant conveyors and every 2 to 3 years on high-speed overland systems. On underground conveyors subject to floor heave, check squareness and level at every scheduled maintenance shift (typically weekly).

Rule of Thumb: Align the weigh idler, the two approach idlers upstream, and the two retreat idlers downstream as a single five-frame precision section. Level all five frames to within 0.5 mm [0.020 in] and limit frame-to-frame steps to 0.3 mm [0.012 in]. If the belt scale drifts out of calibration, re-check these five frames before investigating the load cells or electronics.

Rule of Thumb: A 100 m [330 ft] steel stringer changes length by approximately 12 mm [0.47 in] per 10 deg C [18 deg F] temperature change (coefficient of thermal expansion 12 µm/m per deg C [6.7 µin/in per deg F]). On elevated galleries in direct sun, the top chord heats faster than the bottom chord, bowing the structure (thermal hogging). Midday surveys in direct sun produce different results from surveys at dawn. Take critical measurements at a consistent time when the structure is in thermal equilibrium, before sunrise or at night.

Rule of Thumb: The three idler frames immediately downstream of the loading zone and the three frames immediately upstream of the head pulley set the belt position on the carry run and at the drive. Misalignment at the loading zone tracks the loaded belt off-centre along the carry run. Misalignment at the head pulley causes the belt to enter the drive off-centre, producing uneven belt tension, accelerated pulley lagging wear, and potential single-sided slip. Check these six frames at double the frequency of the rest of the conveyor.

17. Pipe (or Tube) Conveyor Idler Arrangements

A pipe (tube) conveyor forms the flat belt into a fully enclosed tube after the loading point and opens it back to a flat trough at discharge. The belt is enclosed on all sides by idler rolls arranged in a polygonal panel.

17.1 Idler Panel Configurations

  • Hexagonal (6-roll): Six rolls at 60 deg spacing. Common configuration for most pipe diameters. Provides uniform radial support and even contact pressure distribution around the full circumference.
  • Octagonal (8-roll): Eight rolls at 45 deg spacing. Used on some large-diameter designs to reduce pressure per roll and improve pipe circularity.
  • Pentagonal (5-roll) and other configurations: Less common. Used in specialised applications or by specific manufacturers.

17.2 Panel Spacing

Set panel spacing to maintain the circular cross-section against material pressure and the belt's tendency to spring open. Reduce spacing on horizontal and vertical curves.

  • Carry side: 0.8 to 2.0 m [2.6 to 6.6 ft], depending on pipe diameter, belt stiffness, material density, and curve radius.
  • Return side: 1.5 to 4.0 m [5.0 to 13 ft]. The empty return pipe carries no material pressure and is held closed by panel contact alone.
  • Transition zones (forming and opening): 0.5 to 0.8 m [1.6 to 2.6 ft]. Closely spaced panels guide folding and unfolding without wrinkling or kinking the belt.

17.3 Contact Pressure

Unlike a troughed conveyor, where the belt rests on the rolls under gravity, the pipe belt is pressed outward against all surrounding rolls by material pressure and the belt's elastic opening force. Every roll carries positive contact pressure.

Contact pressure determines:

  • Belt cover wear rate. Higher contact pressure accelerates abrasion of the outer belt cover.
  • Rolling resistance. Resistance on each roll increases with contact pressure, and each panel typically contains six or more rolls. Idler resistance per metre is several times that of an equivalent troughed conveyor.
  • Belt overlap integrity. If contact pressure is too low (panels too far apart or too few rolls per panel), the overlap opens and material spills.

17.4 Alignment and Rotation Control

A closed tube can twist about its longitudinal axis. Excessive rotation moves the overlap to the side or bottom, compromising the seal and causing leakage. Rotation is controlled by:

  • Precise panel alignment. Angular misalignment between successive panels induces twisting torque. Alignment tolerances are tighter than on troughed conveyors.
  • Skewed rolls or panels. Selected rolls or panels are deliberately skewed 1 to 3 deg relative to the conveyor centreline to apply corrective torque, particularly on horizontal curves.
  • Overlap monitoring. Inspection windows or electronic overlap sensors confirm the overlap remains in position (typically top-centre).

18. Idler QA/QC and Factory Acceptance Testing

On a long overland conveyor, idler procurement may exceed 10,000 rollers, and a 1% non-conformance rate is then 100 premature field failures. Factory Acceptance Testing (FAT) is the contractual mechanism to verify product meets specification before shipment.

18.1 Pre-Production Requirements

Before volume production, obtain the following from the manufacturer for review and approval:

  • Manufacturing Procedure Specification (MPS) covering shell fabrication (rolling, welding, machining), shaft machining, bearing pressing, seal assembly, grease filling, and end-plate attachment.
  • Welding Procedure Specifications (WPS) and Welding Procedure Qualification Records (WPQR) for all structural welds on the shell and end plates.
  • Material certificates (mill certificates) for the shell tube and shaft bar, and bearing manufacturer certificates of conformance.
  • Grease data sheet confirming grease type, base oil viscosity, thickener type, operating temperature range, and speed factor (n_dm) limit.
  • Seal assembly drawing confirming seal type, material, and dimensional compliance.

18.2 In-Production Testing (Statistical Sampling)

For high-volume production, 100% testing is impractical for most parameters. Apply statistical sampling per the contractual sampling plan, typically AS 1199 / ISO 2859 with an agreed Acceptance Quality Limit (AQL).

Verify dimensions on sampled rollers:

  • Measure shell outside diameter at three points along the face length (both ends and centre) using a calibrated micrometer or laser diameter gauge. Reject rollers with any measurement outside the specified tolerance.
  • Measure shell face length with a vernier caliper against the ISO 1537 / DIN 22107 length for the specified belt width.
  • Measure shaft diameter at the bearing journals with a calibrated micrometer against the bearing manufacturer's shaft tolerance class.

Measure TIR and MIS on sampled rollers:

  • Support the shaft on precision V-blocks and rotate the shell through one revolution against a dial indicator or laser displacement sensor. Record the difference between maximum and minimum readings as TIR.
  • For MIS, record radial displacement at 6 deg increments (60 points per revolution). The maximum change between any two adjacent points is the MIS.
  • Reject rollers where TIR or MIS exceeds the specified limit.

Verify grease fill on sampled rollers. Weigh sampled rollers before and after grease filling, or weigh the grease charge during assembly, and confirm the fill mass is within the manufacturer's specified range. Under-filling reduces bearing life. Over-filling pressurises the seals, increases rotating resistance, and can force grease past the labyrinth.

Test seal integrity on sampled rollers with contact seals. Labyrinth seals pass air by design and are qualified by dust and water ingress type testing. Apply a low-pressure air test (typically 15 to 20 kPa [2 to 3 psi]) to the bearing cavity through a test port. Submerge the roller in water, or coat the seal interface with soap solution, and observe for bubbles. Visible leakage indicates a non-conforming seal assembly.

18.3 100% Testing (Every Roller)

Spin test every roller:

  • Spin each roller on a motorised bench at design RPM (or a standard test speed, typically 500 RPM) for a minimum of 30 seconds and check for excessive noise (bearing defect), vibration (unbalance or TIR), and abnormal resistance (seal binding or bearing misalignment).
  • Where specified, measure rotating resistance torque with a calibrated torque transducer and reject rollers exceeding the specified maximum.
  • After the spin test, allow the roller to coast to a stop. Flag abnormally short free-spin times, which indicate excessive seal friction or bearing defects, for investigation.

Inspect every roller visually and dimensionally:

  • Inspect for weld defects (porosity, undercut, spatter), shell defects (dents, gouges, corrosion), seal damage, and correct marking.
  • Verify overall length and shaft protrusion with a go/no-go gauge or caliper to confirm fit in the specified frame.

18.4 Type Testing (One-Off Qualification)

Type tests qualify each roller design for the application and are repeated only if the design is modified.

Test rotating resistance per DIN 22112-3 / SANS 1313:

  • Mount a production-representative roller on a standardised rig. Measure rotating resistance at design load and speed and compare it against the specified maximum.
  • This verifies that the bearing, seal, and grease combination meets the rotating resistance assumed in the drive calculation.

Test dust and water ingress per DIN 22112-3 / SANS 1313, or the manufacturer's standard:

  • Expose sample rollers to controlled dust and water conditions per the standard, then disassemble them and inspect the bearings for contamination, corrosion, or wear.
  • Standard ingress tests may not represent heavily contaminated sites. Specify enhanced tests (e.g. inclined dust ingress under load) where field contamination exceeds the standard test conditions.

18.5 Documentation and Traceability

Mark each roller with a serial or batch number linking it to its production batch, material certificates, test results, and grease fill record. Traceability supports warranty claims, failure analysis, and life-cycle tracking.

FAT cost is minor compared with replacing non-conforming rollers on a remote site. For procurement exceeding 1,000 rollers, make a formal FAT programme with defined sampling plans, acceptance criteria, and hold and witness points a contractual requirement.

19. Idler Storage, Handling and Shelf Life

Idlers contain factory-filled grease, pressed bearings, and engineered seals. Improper storage, handling, or extended shelf time degrades the roller before installation and is a source of premature failure.

19.1 False Brinelling During Storage and Transport

False brinelling is wear at rolling element contact points on a stationary bearing, caused by micro-vibration. Without rotation there is no elastohydrodynamic film. Static load squeezes grease from the contact, and oscillation produces fretting wear at each ball position. After installation the marks act as stress raisers, producing early noise, vibration, and spalling before the calculated L10h life. This failure can be misdiagnosed as a manufacturing defect.

To reduce false brinelling:

  • Store rollers horizontally on flat, vibration-free surfaces. Do not stand rollers on end, as this concentrates the roller weight on a single bearing.
  • For long road or rail transport, pack rollers in individual cardboard or foam cradles that isolate the bearing housings from vibration.
  • Do not stack rollers more than three layers high without intermediate timber or foam separators.
  • On sites with significant ambient vibration (crushers, screens, rail lines), store rollers in a separate, isolated laydown area.
  • For long-term storage of critical spares, rotate rollers several revolutions at regular intervals to redistribute grease and move rolling element contact positions.

19.2 Grease Degradation During Extended Storage

Grease in a sealed-for-life bearing has a finite shelf life. Base oil separates from the thickener (oil bleed), migrates from the rolling elements, and collects at the lowest point of the cavity. On installation, the bearing can run with inadequate lubrication until the grease redistributes.

Obtain the shelf-life limit from the idler manufacturer for the specified grease. Without manufacturer data, apply the following conservative limits from date of manufacture:

  • Lithium complex grease: 12 months.
  • Synthetic polyurea grease: 18 months.
  • Perfluoropolyether (PFPE) grease: 24 months.

These limits assume dry, shaded, temperature-stable storage at 10 to 30 deg C [50 to 86 deg F]. Direct sun, tropical humidity, or temperatures above 40 deg C [104 deg F] reduce shelf life.

To manage grease shelf life:

  • Apply First-In-First-Out (FIFO) stock rotation.
  • Mark each roller carton with the date of manufacture and the grease type.
  • Verify grease condition before installing rollers beyond their shelf life. Purge and refill re-lubricatable rollers to specification. For sealed-for-life rollers, weigh premature failure risk against replacement cost. Scrap expired sealed rollers on critical conveyors.

19.3 Seal Degradation

Rubber and polyurethane seals degrade in storage through ozone attack, UV exposure, and thermal ageing. Degraded seals crack once the roller rotates, admitting dust and water and causing bearing failure within weeks or months of commissioning.

To reduce seal degradation:

  • Store rollers in their original packaging until installation.
  • Keep rollers out of direct sunlight and away from ozone-generating equipment (electric motors, welders, high-voltage switchgear).

ISO 2230 initial storage periods under controlled conditions are 5 years for polyurethane, 7 years for nitrile (NBR), and 10 years for FKM. For assembled rollers, grease shelf life is normally the governing limit.

19.4 Polymer Shell Creep

HDPE and polymer shells creep (permanently deform under sustained load) in storage. Bulk stacking or point support (e.g. a narrow shelf edge) creates a flat spot that does not recover.

To reduce creep damage:

  • Store polymer rollers in V-cradles or on flat, wide surfaces that support the full face length.
  • Do not stack polymer rollers more than two layers high.
  • Before installing a polymer roller stored for more than 6 months, roll it on a flat surface and check for flat spots or out-of-roundness. Measure TIR on the bench if the roller is for a weigh deck or high-speed application.

Storage damage to bearings, grease and seals is not externally visible. Specify a formal storage and stock management procedure in large idler procurement contracts.

21. Noise and Vibration Considerations

21.1 Sources of Idler Noise

  • Bearing noise: A correctly functioning idler generates minimal noise. As a bearing degrades (spalling, contamination, loss of lubrication), it emits a rumble or squeal that increases in amplitude and broadens in frequency content as the defect progresses.
  • Shell resonance: The hollow steel shell can resonate when excited by belt splice irregularities or lump impacts. Thicker walls reduce shell response. Damped shells (polymer or rubber-lagged) reduce resonance further.
  • Junction noise (roller gap): The belt cover deflects into the gap between centre and wing rolls and rebounds, producing a periodic popping noise on fast, lightly loaded belts. Offset idlers or minimal gap widths reduce junction noise.

21.2 Noise Mitigation

  • Specify larger roller diameters. Lower rotational speed reduces bearing and shell excitation.
  • Specify HDPE or polymer shell idlers for return runs. Polymer shells damp resonance more effectively than steel tubes.
  • Use rubber disc return idlers. The spaced rubber rings break up shell resonance and reduce contact noise.
  • Specify low TIR. An out-of-round roller generates a periodic thump every revolution. Specify TIR less than 0.5 mm [0.020 in] on rollers in noise-sensitive corridors and less than 0.3 mm [0.012 in] on rollers adjacent to populated areas.
  • Install noise barriers or earth bunds along overland conveyor corridors in noise-sensitive zones where idler noise cannot be reduced sufficiently at source.

22. Idler Maintenance, Inspection and Failure Modes

Replace any idler roller that exhibits a seized bearing, shell wear exceeding 2 mm [0.08 in], visible shaft deflection, or TIR greater than the purchase specification limit. On belt-scale (weigh) idlers, apply the scale manufacturer's TIR limit, typically 0.38 mm [0.015 in].

22.1 Common Failure Modes

  • Seized (locked) rollers: Bearing seizes from contamination, lubrication failure, or fatigue. The belt slides over the stationary roller, wearing a flat spot on the shell and grinding the bottom cover. On fast, heavily loaded belts, frictional heat can ignite the belt and is a recognised cause of underground conveyor fires.
  • Shell wear (grooving): On return idlers in particular, abrasive carryback on the dirty side of the belt grinds a circumferential groove into the steel shell. Once the shell wall is worn through, the exposed shell edge cuts the belt.
  • Bearing spalling: Rolling contact fatigue cracks initiate at or below the bearing raceway surface and propagate, causing pitting and rough running with increasing noise and vibration before seizure. Spalling is accelerated by overloading, misalignment, and contamination.
  • Shaft bending: Excessive concentrated load (e.g. large lump impact) permanently bends the idler shaft. The stationary shaft then holds the bearings out of alignment, producing edge loading, vibration and accelerated bearing fatigue, and the tilted roll axis degrades belt tracking.

22.2 Inspection Methods

  • Visual inspection: Walk the conveyor to identify seized rollers (flat spots, discolouration, smoke, abnormal noise), shell wear, and frame damage. This is the baseline method. Set the interval by risk assessment and statutory requirements.
  • Acoustic monitoring: Handheld ultrasonic detectors or fixed microphone arrays detect high-frequency bearing defect signatures before seizure. Fixed systems suit long conveyors with large idler populations.
  • Thermal imaging: Infrared cameras identify hot rollers (indicating a bearing that is binding or has seized) from a distance, which suits high-speed conveyors where close access is hazardous. Temperature rise occurs late in bearing degradation, so thermal imaging confirms rather than predicts failure.
  • Vibration analysis: Accelerometers mounted on the idler frame detect bearing defect frequencies (outer race, inner race, rolling element, and cage). Typically reserved for belt-scale idlers and high-value installations.

23. Lifecycle Cost Analysis Framework

Higher-specification idlers cost more at purchase (CAPEX) but can deliver lower operating expenditure (OPEX) through extended bearing life, reduced power consumption, fewer replacements, and less unplanned downtime.

23.1 Cost Components

  • Capital Cost (CAPEX): Unit purchase price of each roller (including bearing, seal, shaft, shell, and grease). Unit purchase price of each idler frame (including corrosion protection). Freight cost (driven by roller weight and site remoteness). Installation labour cost (driven by roller weight, frame complexity, and access difficulty).
  • Energy Cost: Power demand attributable to idler rotating resistance, calculated from measured roller rotating resistance, roller quantity, and belt speed, multiplied by annual operating hours and energy price (AUD/kWh or local currency). This cost accumulates over the full design life and is most significant on long overland conveyors.
  • Replacement Cost (Planned): The number of roller replacements expected over the design life, based on the calculated L10h bearing life, grease life, and shell wear life, multiplied by the unit cost of each replacement roller plus labour. Labour includes belt lifting, roller extraction, new roller insertion, and belt lowering, and is influenced by roller weight (manual handling or mechanical aids) and site access (surface plant, underground, or elevated gallery).
  • Unplanned Downtime Cost: The cost per hour of unplanned conveyor stoppage (lost production revenue, contractual penalties, demurrage charges) multiplied by the expected frequency and duration of unplanned stoppages caused by idler failures (seized rollers, belt damage from locked rollers, fire incidents). This cost is difficult to quantify but can dominate total cost of ownership (TCO) on high-utilisation conveyors without redundancy (e.g. port ship-loading systems).
  • Spare Parts Inventory Cost: Capital tied up in spare roller and frame inventory, storage cost (warehouse space, stock management labour), and shelf-life expiry of stored rollers (grease degradation, seal ageing).

23.2 Comparative Analysis Method

Calculate the TCO of each idler specification (e.g. standard versus premium) over the conveyor design life (typically 20 to 30 years for a permanent installation).

  1. Calculate CAPEX for each option (unit price multiplied by total quantity, plus freight and installation).
  2. Calculate annual energy cost for each option. Do not represent seal options with different DIN 22101 artificial friction factors (f). The factor f also includes belt indentation and flexure losses that do not depend on idler specification.
  3. Calculate planned replacement cost for each option. Divide the design life (in operating hours) by the expected replacement interval (the shortest of L10h, grease life, and shell wear life) to determine the number of replacement cycles. Multiply by the unit replacement cost (parts plus labour). L10h is the life exceeded by 90% of bearings, so using it as the interval overstates replacement quantity. Apply it consistently across all options.
  4. Estimate unplanned downtime cost for each option using historical failure rate data from comparable installations, or a reliability model based on the calculated L10h and environmental factors.
  5. Discount annual costs to present value at the owner's discount rate, including energy price escalation, then sum all cost components for each option. The option with the lowest TCO is the economically optimal specification, regardless of which option has the lowest CAPEX.

23.3 Sensitivity Analysis

Vary key inputs (energy price +/-30%, replacement labour +/-50%, discount rate, downtime cost, and failure rate) and confirm the option ranking holds. If ranking reverses under plausible variations, treat the options as economically equivalent and select on non-cost criteria (reliability, standardisation, vendor support).

On a long overland conveyor operating for 20 years, the energy cost difference between f = 0.020 and f = 0.030 can be a substantial proportion of the capital cost of the entire idler population. Idler rotating resistance is one contributor to this range, together with belt indentation, flexure and alignment.

Note: Indicative t_contact values: rigid steel idler 0.01 to 0.05 s, rubber-lagged impact idler 0.05 to 0.15 s, impact cradle 0.10 to 0.30 s. For a system of known stiffness k (N/m), t_contact = (PI / 2) * SQRT(m_lump / k), where PI = 3.14159. Doubling the contact time halves the impact force. Rubber-lagged impact idlers and impact cradles do not reduce the impact energy. They extend the contact duration, which reduces the peak force on the bearings and belt carcass.

Engineering Takeaway: Reduce impact force by extending contact duration (rubber lagging, impact cradles) and by minimising free-fall height through chute design.

Note: Typical grease speed limits are:

  • Standard mineral grease (lithium complex): n_dm less than or equal to 300,000 to 350,000 mm/min
  • Polyurea thickened grease: n_dm less than or equal to 500,000 to 600,000 mm/min
  • High-speed synthetic (ester or polyalphaolefin (PAO) base): n_dm of 800,000 mm/min or greater

Engineering Takeaway: The grease speed limit rarely governs idler roller diameter. Use this check to confirm grease suitability for small rollers at high speed and for non-standard bearings.

Note: If the grease life is less than the L10h target, the bearing is likely to fail from lubricant degradation before mechanical fatigue. Specify a re-lubrication system, a higher-temperature synthetic grease, or a larger roller diameter to reduce RPM. For sealed-for-life bearings, confirm the result against the bearing manufacturer's grease life data for capped bearings.

Example: A 6205 bearing (25 mm [0.98 in] bore) at 503 RPM and 70 deg C [158 deg F]: t_grease = 1.0 * 10 * ((14,000,000 / (503 * 5)) - 4 * 25) = 10 * (5,567 - 100) = 54,700 hours. At 85 deg C [185 deg F], this halves to 27,300 hours.

Engineering Takeaway: Grease life, rather than fatigue life, can limit sealed-for-life idler bearings, particularly above 70 deg C [158 deg F].

If this is not met, reduce the unsupported face length, increase the shell diameter or use a material with a higher E / rho ratio. For identical geometry, an HDPE shell has approximately one fifth of the critical speed of a steel shell.

Example: A steel shell 152 mm [6 in] in diameter with a 4.5 mm [0.18 in] wall and 465 mm [18.3 in] face length: I_shell = 5.68 * 10^-6 m4, A_shell = 2.09 * 10^-3 m2 and f_critical = 1,960 Hz, giving N_critical of approximately 117,600 RPM. This is more than 200 times the 503 RPM of the same roller at 4.0 m/s [787 fpm]. The check is relevant to long-face polymer rolls on wide, high-speed belts.

Engineering Takeaway: Critical speed is proportional to the shell radius of gyration, SQRT(D^2 + D_i^2) / 4, and inversely proportional to the square of the unsupported face length. Wall thickness has little effect because shell stiffness and mass increase together. Polymer rollers require a critical speed check due to their low modulus.
