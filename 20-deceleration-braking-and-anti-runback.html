1. Deceleration, Braking, and Anti-Runback

On drive motor de-energisation, the elevation profile governs the dynamic response.

Horizontal conveyors coast to rest, inclined conveyors decelerate and run back where the gravitational force on the loaded belt exceeds running resistance, and declined conveyors accelerate where the gravitational force exceeds running resistance.

2. Coasting and Transfer Chutes

Coasting time is proportional to system inertia and belt speed, and inversely proportional to the net retarding force from running resistance and gravity.

Keep the receiving conveyor running until the feeding conveyor stops discharging. Otherwise, material discharges onto a stationary belt and blocks the transfer chute. Restarting a buried receiving belt increases breakaway torque and risks drive pulley slip, cover damage and splice overload.

Where the feeding conveyor coasts longer than the receiving conveyor, delay the receiving conveyor stop through the control sequence for controlled stops. For power loss, specify a brake on the feeding conveyor or install a flywheel on the receiving conveyor.

Size the downstream chute for the material discharged after a stop command under power loss and emergency stop cases.

3. Brake Application Delay

Following a trip or power loss, a spring-applied brake develops torque after a delay while control relays open, hydraulic dump valves de-energise, fluid evacuates the calliper lines and springs close the rotor air gap.

This sequence typically takes 0.5 to 1.5 s. Obtain the value for the specific brake and hydraulic circuit from the supplier. The conveyor continues to discharge during this delay. Include this travel distance in transfer chute capacity calculations.

4. Anti-Runback Devices

Fit an anti-runback device where the net runback force is positive in any load case. Calculate runback force using the lowest credible friction factor. Unrestrained runback causes material spillage at the tail pulley, transfer chute blockages and driveline damage from reverse overspeed.

4.2 Sizing the Anti-Runback Device

When drive power is removed under load, the belt decelerates and gravity reverses it. The stretched belt recoils during holdback engagement and generates a transient tension wave. Locking against this wave produces a snap-back torque greater than the static runback torque. Calculate the dynamic runback torque by applying a dynamic multiplier to the static runback torque, or by dynamic analysis.

AS/NZS 4024.3610 sets the general requirements for systems that stop and hold a conveyor, and AS/NZS 4024.3611 adds requirements specific to belt conveyors. Rate each anti-runback device to stop and hold at least 150% of the maximum dynamic runback torque in the worst-case load case. Assess abnormal dynamic effects and conveyor overload.

Install redundant anti-runback systems where risk assessment identifies runback as a hazard to personnel or critical infrastructure. Redundant configurations include two identical mechanical holdbacks on the drive pulley shaft extensions, or one mechanical holdback paired with an independent fail-safe holding brake such as a spring-applied, hydraulically released low-speed calliper disc brake on the pulley. Rate each device independently to the 150% requirement.

4.3 Multiple Anti-Runbacks and Load Sharing

On large dual-drive incline conveyors, the required holding torque can exceed the capacity of a single commercially available holdback. Do not split the required holding torque between passive mechanical holdbacks on physically adjacent drive pulleys.

Manufacturing tolerances and backlash stagger the engagement of sprag and cam clutches. The short belt span between adjacent drive pulleys provides minimal compliance. The first holdback to lock, sized for 50% of the load, absorbs the full snap-back load and overloads. If it fails, the full load transfers to the second holdback and can cause cascade failure.

Different device types may be installed on adjacent drive pulleys where engagement is sequential. A mechanical holdback arrests reverse rotation first. A fail-safe brake, sized to arrest and hold the full load independently, sets at zero speed and holds the belt if the holdback fails. Alternatively, specify torque-limiting holdbacks, which slip at a set torque and promote load sharing.

4.4 Anti-Runback Maintenance and Mechanical Disablement

Belt replacement and splice repairs can require reverse belt movement, which the anti-runback device prevents on an inclined conveyor.

Anti-runback devices can incorporate a mechanical override operated by lever, screw jack or hydraulic actuator. The override retracts the sprags or rollers from the race, lifts the pawl in ratchet mechanisms or releases holding torque by hydraulic pressure in brake-based systems, placing the device in a bidirectional freewheeling state.

Secure the belt before releasing the device. Apply belt clamps rated for the maximum runback tension, and ground and lock the counterweight where the work requires tension release. Releasing the device without securing the belt can cause uncontrolled runback.

Do not operate the conveyor with the device released. Before every start, verify that the engagement indicator shows the device engaged. Interlock the override with the conveyor start permissive where practicable.

5. Braking Systems

Brakes are required to stop a conveyor faster than its natural coasting time, to prevent a declined conveyor from running away, and to hold the belt at standstill.

All mechanical conveyor brakes must be fail-safe. Springs apply the brake and electrical, hydraulic or pneumatic power releases it. Loss of power, a blown fuse or a burst hydraulic hose applies the brake.

Size brake torque from the loads on the conveyor, not from the drive motor rating. A brake rated as a multiple of motor torque can under-brake a declined conveyor, where the regenerative load plus the torque needed to decelerate the moving mass exceeds that multiple. It can also over-brake a horizontal conveyor, where the excess torque raises stopping tension, risks slip at the braked pulley and can lift the belt at concave curves.

5.1 Split-Location Braking Risks

Avoid distributing fixed-torque mechanical brakes across separated pulleys e.g. head and tail pulleys to share thermal load, unless dynamic analysis verifies the tension response for every load case and brake timing tolerance.

On undulating profiles, material distribution determines whether the belt decelerates or accelerates during a stop, but spring-applied brakes apply constant torque. Simultaneous application of separated brakes generates transient stress waves, slack belt in low-tension zones and carcass overload.

Differential pad wear changes application delay and torque. This concentrates energy in one brake until it fails and transfers the full load to the remaining brake.

Alternatively, use VFD braking to modulate torque to suit the changing load profile.

Where mechanical braking is required on power loss, locate the brakes at a single pulley at the optimal braking location e.g. the tail pulley of a declined conveyor. A single location applies uniform torque and avoids asynchronous brake interaction.

5.2 Brake Thermal Capacity and Duty Cycle

Mechanical brakes convert kinetic and potential energy to heat. Excess temperature causes rotor distortion and brake fade.

On declined conveyors, released potential energy often exceeds system kinetic energy. Brakes on regenerative conveyors therefore require greater thermal mass than brakes on equivalent horizontal conveyors.

Size brake thermal capacity for the worst-case duty cycle and for a minimum of three consecutive full-load emergency stops from rated speed without cooling. Keep the rotor temperature after the third stop below the pad manufacturer's limit. Otherwise, increase rotor mass, upgrade the material grade or add forced-air cooling.

5.3 Brake Pad Material Selection

The friction coefficient varies with temperature, sliding speed, contact pressure and contamination by water, dust or oil. Specify pad and rotor combinations with tested friction data across the operating envelope.

5.4 Brake Rotor Material Selection

Specify grey cast iron (e.g. AS 1830 grade JL/250 / EN-GJL-250, formerly GG-25) for standard rotors for its thermal conductivity and vibration damping. Specify forged or cast steel rotors (e.g. AS 1442 Grade 1040) for heavy-duty applications above 400 deg C [750 deg F] or where thermal cracking of cast iron is a risk.

Friction data applies only to the pad and rotor combination tested. Do not substitute rotor material without supplier confirmation of the friction coefficient.

6. Flywheel and Inertia Control

Rotating components (motor rotors, couplings, gearbox internals and pulleys), together with belt and material mass, determine system inertia. On complex profiles, inherent inertia can be insufficient to prevent transient tension collapse during an uncontrolled stop. Add a flywheel to increase system inertia.

A typical application is a predominantly horizontal conveyor terminating in a steep incline to the head pulley e.g. an overland conveyor rising to a discharge structure. On power loss, gravity decelerates the loaded incline while the horizontal section maintains forward momentum. Carry-side tension at the base of the incline collapses. The belt sags, folds at the concave curve and spills material. Installing a head drive flywheel extends the deceleration period and maintains positive tension until the horizontal section stops.

6.1 Installation Location

Flywheel kinetic energy is proportional to its mass moment of inertia and the square of its rotational speed. Speed therefore has greater influence than inertia.

A flywheel on the low-speed pulley shaft e.g. 50 rpm requires large mass, typically with structural reinforcement and larger bearings.

A flywheel on the high-speed shaft e.g. 1,500 rpm typically rotates 30 to 46 times faster than the pulley. Reflected inertia scales with the square of the speed ratio, so the high-speed flywheel requires approximately 1/900 to 1/2,100 of the inertia of an equivalent low-speed flywheel.

Example: At a gear ratio of 30, a pulley-shaft inertia of 2,500 kg m2 [59,300 lb ft2] divided by 900 (the ratio squared) equals 2.8 kg m2 [66 lb ft2] at the motor shaft.

Check motor starting duty, coupling and gearbox input ratings for the added flywheel inertia. A high-speed flywheel acts on the belt only while the gearbox is intact.

6.2 Flywheel and Brake Pairing

Adding a flywheel extends coasting time. Pair the flywheel with a controlled mechanical brake that applies a retarding force to stop the system within the required time. Set brake torque and application timing by dynamic analysis so carry-side tension at the concave curve remains positive.

6.3 Flywheel Hazards and Chute Capacity

A flywheel extends run-on time after a stop. Include run-on time in the guarding and access risk assessment. Size the receiving transfer chute for the extended coasting volume.

Treat flywheels as stored energy sources. A driveline failure decouples the flywheel from the belt load, leaving it coasting at near operating speed.

Enclose flywheels in heavy-gauge steel guards. Monitor shaft bearings with continuous vibration sensors to detect degradation. Include flywheel run-down in isolation procedures and confirm zero speed before access.

7. Regenerative (Downhill) Conveyors

A conveyor is regenerative where the net gravitational force on the material exceeds running resistance. Assess every load case, since partial loading can make a conveyor regenerative.

Treat a loaded declined conveyor as a stored-energy system. Without restraint, the belt accelerates toward a terminal velocity defined by gradient, moving mass and running resistance, which can exceed idler, pulley and belt design limits.

7.1 Braking Redundancy

Provide independent primary and secondary braking systems. The primary system may be a non-fail-safe controlled braking system, such as VFD braking. The secondary brake must be fail-safe, independent and rated to stop and hold at least 150% of the maximum dynamic regenerative load per AS/NZS 4024.3610.

7.2 Transfer Point Overload

On overspeed, material overshoots the designed impact zone and exceeds the capacity of the receiving equipment. This causes chute impact damage, plugging, spillage and belt damage.

Establish exclusion zones at regenerative conveyor discharge areas unless the conveyor is fitted with a redundant fail-safe mechanical brake.

8. Stop Sequencing and Geometry-Specific Control

Distinguish in the control logic between a Category 1 stop, where the VFD ramps the belt to zero speed on an S-curve before removing power, and a Category 0 stop, where power is removed immediately and mechanical brakes apply. Power loss produces an uncontrolled stop equivalent to Category 0. Emergency stops and pull-wire trips may be Category 0 or Category 1 as determined by risk assessment.

The conveyor profile determines VFD torque commands and brake timing.

9. Deceleration Limits for Personnel Safety

For conveyors with adjacent personnel access during operation, limit the emergency deceleration rate to 0.3 m/s2 [0.98 ft/s2] unless a site-specific risk assessment demonstrates otherwise.

On man-riding conveyors, limit deceleration to prevent riders sliding into fixed equipment. Apply the limit set by the applicable mining regulation and risk assessment. Use 0.1 to 0.15 m/s2 [0.33 to 0.49 ft/s2] as an indicative design range.

Where the deceleration needed to prevent runaway on a declined conveyor exceeds the personnel limit, prevent exposure with interlocked access gates or man-riding lockout so riders are not present when high deceleration rates apply.
