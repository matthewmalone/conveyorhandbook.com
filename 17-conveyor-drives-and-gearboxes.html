1. Conveyor Drives and Gearboxes

This chapter covers gearbox selection, drive mounting, shaft connections, service and thermal ratings, lubrication, procurement specification, spares and failure diagnosis for conveyor drives.

5. Torque Arms and Reaction Systems

On shaft-mounted drives, the torque arm transfers the gearbox housing reaction torque to the conveyor frame, preventing housing rotation.

5.1 Reaction Torque

The torque arm, its lug and its anchorage must be rated for the peak motor torque multiplied by the gear ratio. Take peak motor torque as the breakdown torque for Direct On-Line (DOL) starting, the drive torque limit for Variable Frequency Drive (VFD) starting, or the coupling stall torque for fluid couplings. Designing solely for running conditions risks structural failure during locked-rotor or belt jam events.

5.2 End Connections

Use articulating torque arm end connections to accommodate angular displacement induced by shaft deflection, thermal expansion, and structural deformation. Rigid connections induce bending moments in the arm and contribute to fatigue failure of mounting lugs.

Specify the torque arm lug as an Original Equipment Manufacturer (OEM) machined component rated for the peak torque. Do not field-weld lugs to housings. Welding introduces localised heat-affected zones and can distort bearing bores.

5.3 Failure Consequences

Torque arm failure results in unrestrained gearbox rotation, causing peripheral equipment damage. On inclined conveyors, loss of the torque arm can allow loaded belt runback where the backstop is internal to the gearbox, because the backstop reacts through the housing. Include all torque arm components in the preventive maintenance inspection schedule.

6. Mechanical Service Factors

Apply a mechanical service factor to the nominal motor rating when selecting the gearbox, to accommodate dynamic shock loads, starting torque transients, and fatigue. Calculate gear tooth bending and contact stress ratings to ISO 6336 / American Gear Manufacturers Association (AGMA) 2001 for helical stages, and ISO 10300 / AGMA 2003 for bevel stages. Calculate bearing fatigue life to ISO 281 (L10m modified rating life method). The service factor covers the dynamic load spectrum not represented by the nominal motor rating.

7. Thermal Ratings and Lubricant Selection

Lubricant thermal degradation is a common gearbox failure mode in heavy-duty service and typically precedes mechanical tooth breakage. Evaluate gearboxes against both a mechanical rating (torque capacity) and a thermal rating (heat dissipation capacity before lubricant degradation). For a given heat dissipation capacity, the lubricant temperature limit sets the gearbox thermal limit.

Limit continuous sump temperature to 90 deg C [194 deg F] for mineral gear oils. Exceeding this threshold accelerates oxidation and reduces viscosity and film thickness, increasing gear wear (scuffing and micropitting). Synthetic Polyalphaolefin (PAO) and Polyalkylene Glycol (PAG) gear oils operate continuously at sump temperatures of 100 to 110 deg C [212 to 230 deg F] while maintaining operational viscosity, increasing the gearbox thermal rating.

In high-ambient environments, temperatures can exceed 45 deg C [113 deg F], compounded by direct solar radiation.

Include direct solar gain in the effective ambient temperature. For exposed or dark-finished casings, apply an allowance of 10 to 15 deg C [18 to 27 deg F] above maximum ambient temperature. For casings with reflective paint or sun shields, apply an allowance of 5 to 10 deg C [9 to 18 deg F]. Confirm the allowance against site solar irradiance data (W/m2).

8. Cooling Methods

Apply a thermal safety factor of at least 1.2. Where the safety factor falls below 1.2, upsize the gearbox frame or add active cooling. Extended low-speed operation through fluid couplings or VFD jogging generates continuous thermal load with reduced fan cooling, and requires an increased thermal safety factor.

Often unsuitable for VFD-controlled drives with extended or frequent low-speed operation. Introduces a minor parasitic power loss. Requires fin cleaning and guarding.

Increases oil volume and oil change cost. On shaft-mounted drives, increases overhung load on the pulley shaft.

9. Gearbox Thermal Safety Factors

Evaluate motor and gearbox thermal limits separately. The gearbox thermal safety factor confirms that the casing surface area and auxiliary cooling systems can dissipate heat generated by friction, churning losses and prolonged starting loads. It is the ratio of the thermal rating, corrected for ambient temperature and cooling method, to the transmitted power.

10. Gearbox Housing Design and Mounting Interfaces

Housing design dictates field maintainability, thermal dissipation, and rigging requirements.

10.1 Breathers and Pressure Relief

Unvented housings develop positive pressure during heating, compromising shaft seals, and negative pressure during cooling, drawing in atmospheric contaminants.

Fit all conveyor gearbox housings with a breather valve. In mining applications, fit a desiccant breather to limit moisture ingress. Install it at the highest point, shielded from washdown.

10.2 Centralised Lubrication Connections

On large drive stations, connect lubrication points by stainless steel or nylon tubing to a centralised panel for single-point servicing outside the guards.

11.1 Viscosity Grade Selection

Select the oil viscosity grade to maintain adequate film thickness at operating temperature. Insufficient viscosity causes boundary lubrication and scuffing. Excessive viscosity increases churning losses and oil temperature.

ISO Viscosity Grade (VG) is specified at 40 deg C [104 deg F] per ISO 3448. Synthetic lubricants have a higher viscosity index, allowing a lower ISO VG grade to achieve required operating viscosity at elevated sump temperatures and reduce churning losses.

11.2 Oil Change Intervals

Oil change intervals depend on lubricant type, operating temperature, and contamination.

Rule of Thumb: Every 10 deg C [18 deg F] increase in sustained operating temperature approximately doubles the oil oxidation rate and halves effective lubricant life (Arrhenius relationship).

11.3 Filtration Requirements (Forced-Circulation Systems)

Fit forced-circulation gearboxes with inline filtration to protect internal components from abrasive wear. Target oil cleanliness to ISO 4406 of 17/15/12 for standard gearboxes and 16/14/11 for fine-pitch gears. Specify filter elements with a beta ratio beta_10 greater than or equal to 75 (absolute filtration rating of 10 µm [0.39 mil] or less). Fit the filter housing with a differential pressure indicator or switch that signals before the element reaches its dirt-holding capacity.

12.1 Documentation Deliverables

Minimum documentation deliverables include:

  • Certified General Arrangement (GA) drawing detailing external dimensions, shaft centreline heights, mounting patterns, and lifting points.
  • Gear rating and bearing life calculations verifying mechanical and thermal capacities against the specified service factors and ambient conditions.
  • FAT report incorporating no-load run results, vibration data, oil leakage inspections, and bevel-gear tooth contact pattern records.
  • Operation and maintenance manual specifying lubrication protocols, fluid capacities, exchange intervals, and critical fastener torques.
  • Recommended spare parts list with part numbers and lead times.
  • Material mill certificates for all gear blanks and shafts, traceable to the heat number (optional).
  • Heat treatment certificates validating hardness, carburised case depth, and mechanical properties (optional).

Audio: A deep, low-frequency rumble that increases in volume over weeks/months. Oil Analysis: Flaking produces distinct spherical or fatigue-chunk wear debris.Replace bearings. If premature, calculate dynamic equivalent load based on field conditions. Verify external overhung loads (OHL) are within allowable limits.
Bearing Cage FailureRetainer/cage separating rolling elements becomes deformed or fractured.Caused by lubrication starvation, excessive vibration, rapid acceleration/deceleration, or skidding (rolling elements slide rather than roll due to inadequate minimum load).Vibration: Sudden, erratic increase in broadband high-frequency vibration. Loss of bearing defect frequency peaks as geometry collapses. Fundamental Train Frequency (FTF) may appear in early stages. Audio: High-pitched squealing, rattling or clicking. Oil Analysis: Brass, bronze, or soft steel debris (depending on cage material) in the filter or sump.Ensure adequate oil flow/splash reaches the highest bearings. Review VFD ramp times to prevent sudden deceleration. Ensure correct bearing preload to prevent skidding in low-load conditions.
Electrical Pitting (Fluting)Formation of microscopic melt craters or rhythmic, washboard ridges (fluting) on bearing raceways and rolling elements.High-frequency common-mode voltages induced by Variable Frequency Drive (VFD) Pulse Width Modulation (PWM). When shaft voltage exceeds oil film dielectric strength, an electrical arc discharges to ground through the bearing, melting the steel.Visual: Washboard patterns on the bearing outer or inner races. A darkened, burnt grease or oil adjacent the bearing. Vibration: High-frequency peaks at bearing defect frequencies (BPFO/BPFI). Oil Analysis: Microscopic spherical steel particles (weld spatter) in oil or filter.Install shaft grounding rings on the motor. Ensure equipotential bonding straps bridge the motor and gearbox housings. Use electrically insulated bearings (ceramic-coated rings or hybrid ceramic elements) for high-risk installations.
False Brinelling (Standby Fretting Wear)Depressions or wear marks matching the pitch of rolling elements on bearing raceways, occurring while the gearbox is stationary.Ambient structural vibration (from adjacent running conveyors or crushers) transmitted to a stationary, non-rotating gearbox. Microscopic movement depletes the EHL oil film, causing metal-to-metal fretting wear between rollers and races.Visual: Oval indentations on the raceway that resemble impact dents. Original grinding marks are worn away inside the dent, unlike true brinelling, where plastic deformation leaves them visible. Vibration: Bearing noise and defect frequencies upon the first startup.Periodically rotate the shafts of standby gearboxes (minimum of 5 revolutions every 3 to 6 months). Mechanically isolate standby drives from structural vibration.

14.3 Shaft and Assembly Failures

Table 15: Shaft and Assembly Failure Modes
Failure Mode Description Root Cause Diagnostic Evidence Corrective Action
Shaft Fatigue at KeywaysCrack initiating at the keyway bottom corner (fillet), propagating through the cross-section until separation. Torsional fatigue cracks typically grow at a 45 deg angle and bending fatigue cracks grow radially.High localised stress concentration (K_t) at the keyway root combined with cyclic dynamic loading (reversing loads, heavy starts, overhung torque arm loads).Non-Destructive Testing (NDT): Magnetic particle or ultrasonic testing shows crack initiation. Vibration: Shift in 1x rpm phase angle and increase in 1x and 2x rpm amplitudes as shaft stiffness changes. Visual: Micro-fretting or rust around the keyway prior to failure.Replace keyed connections with compression couplings (shrink discs) to remove stress concentrations. Where keyways are necessary, use maximum possible keyway fillet radii.
Fretting Corrosion (Hollow Bore Seizure)Generation of red/brown iron oxide powder at the gearbox hollow bore and pulley shaft interface, eventually cold-welding them together.Micro-motion (slip) between the shaft and the bore under cyclic, reversing, or shock loading. Caused by under-torqued shrink discs or excessive diametric clearance in keyed hollow bores.Visual: Rust or red powder extruding from the bore-shaft joint. Maintenance Event: Seizure during scheduled removal, requiring destructive cutting, lancing, or high-tonnage hydraulic pullers.Follow shrink-disc cleaning and torquing procedures. Apply anti-fretting assembly paste (molybdenum disulphide) to non-clamping zones only. Consider flanged rigid couplings for reversing or shock-loaded applications.
Housing Distortion (Soft Foot / Twist)Internal gear shaft misalignment caused by cast housing distortion.Bolting a base-mounted gearbox to an uneven foundation without proper shimming (soft foot), or baseplate deflection under full running torque.Visual: Tooth contact patterns show loading concentrated on one edge of the face width. Mechanical: Dial indicator shows vertical movement when holding-down bolts are loosened. Vibration: 1x and 2x rpm peaks mimicking shaft-coupling misalignment, but internally generated.Perform precision soft-foot checks before final torquing, and correct soft foot to less than 0.05 mm [0.002 in]. Use laser alignment with precision stainless-steel shims, machined steel chocks, or poured epoxy grout to ensure a planar mounting surface. Stiffen baseplate steelwork to limit deflection under running torque.

14.4 Lubrication, Contamination, and Thermal Failures

Failure Mode Description Root Cause Diagnostic Evidence Corrective Action
Seal-Driven ContaminationIngress of atmospheric dust, abrasive bulk materials, or water past shaft seals into the gearbox sump.Worn elastomer seals, grooved shaft surfaces under seal lip, failure to purge labyrinth cavities, or high-pressure washdowns directed at seals. Abrasive Wear (Ploughing / Scoring)Removal of base metal from gear tooth flanks and bearing raceways, leaving a dull, lapped appearance or parallel scratches in the direction of sliding.Hard particulate contamination bypassing seals or breathers and suspending in the lubricant. Conduct a complete gearbox flush and oil replacement to remove suspended abrasives.
Oil Aeration and FoamingEntrapment of air bubbles within the lubricant, creating a persistent foam that cannot dissipate before entering gear meshes or oil pumps.Caused by overfilling the oil sump (resulting in excess churning), water contamination altering the surface tension, or mixing incompatible oil chemistries, which destroys anti-foam additives.Visual: Bubbles or foam in the sight glass. Oil may vent out of the breather valve. Instrumentation: Sudden spikes in bearing and sump temperature, as air bubbles cannot carry loads and act as thermal insulators. Audio: Cavitation noise (a crackling or gravel-like sound) if using an external forced-lubrication pump.Verify oil level adheres to the static fill line. Drain excess if overfilled. Perform a crackle test or Karl Fischer analysis to check for water ingress. Flush and replace oil if additives are depleted.
Thermal RunawayProgressive rise in gearbox operating temperature leading to rapid chemical degradation of the lubricant, loss of EHL film, and subsequent internal mechanical component failure.Imbalance between heat generation and heat dissipation. Common triggers include ambient temperatures exceeding thermal ratings, thick layers of dust/spillage insulating the casing, failure of a forced-lube pump, clogged external heat exchangers, or internal churning losses from overfilling.Instrumentation: Sump temperature sensor alarm. Visual: Blistering or discoloration of the external casing paint. Oil level sight glass showing blackened, thick oil. Oil Analysis: Depletion of oxidation inhibitors, a rise in the Acid Number (AN), and altered kinematic viscosity (either thickened from oxidation or thinned from thermal cracking).Keep external casings free of bulk material buildup. Verify thermal safety factors match ambient conditions. Upgrade to synthetic lubricants to increase the thermal ceiling. If passive cooling is insufficient, install an active external cooling circuit. Maintain oil level at the static fill line to minimise churning friction.
