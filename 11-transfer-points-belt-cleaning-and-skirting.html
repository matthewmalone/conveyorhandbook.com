1. Transfer Points, Belt Cleaning, and Skirting

Conveyor belt damage, dust generation, and spillage originate primarily at transfer points through impact and abrasion. Improper skirting and inadequate load support damage the belt top cover, and ineffective belt cleaners permit carryback, which damages return idlers and causes mistracking.

2. Transfer Chute Design and Material Trajectory

Transfer chute design dictates top cover wear, dust generation, material degradation, and flow continuity. Centre-load bulk material at a velocity and direction matching the receiving belt.

2.1 Material Trajectory Calculations

Discharged bulk material follows an approximately parabolic trajectory governed by gravity, belt speed, pulley radius and material bed depth. If the impact wall is too close to the head pulley, material ricochets backward onto the belt cleaners. If it is too far, material misses the wear liners and wears the unlined chute wall.

2.3 Discrete Element Method Simulation

Analytical 2D trajectory models have limited accuracy for high-speed chute design because bulk material behaves as discrete interacting particles. DEM uses 3D computational models of particle kinematics, including gravity, wall friction, restitution and moisture cohesion.

Use DEM analysis for the following:

  • Wear Prediction: Generates wear maps of peak impact and sliding abrasion to guide liner selection and placement.
  • Volumetric Capacity Verification: Confirms the chute geometry passes the maximum volumetric surge without bridging or blockage.
  • Velocity Alignment: Sets internal deflector angles to align the exit velocity vector with the receiving belt, reducing cover wear and material turbulence.
  • Centreline Loading: Confirms symmetrical material distribution on the receiving belt, reducing mistracking and spillage.
  • Flow Continuity: Identifies deceleration zones where material pools. Correcting the wall angles maintains forward momentum and reduces abrasive wear on the receiving belt.

3. Material Degradation and Attrition

Friable bulk materials (e.g. coal, calcined coke, chemical granules, sintered ore and agricultural pellets) degrade during transfer, and the fines generated by high-velocity impact reduce product yield, increase dust generation and degrade downstream process efficiency. Degradation correlates directly with particle impact velocity at the chute wall or receiving belt, which is governed by discharge velocity and vertical drop height.

Published indices (e.g. the Hardgrove Grindability Index or the brittleness test friability value) indicate material susceptibility to degradation. Establish the permissible drop height by site-specific drop testing of product samples. In the absence of test data, typical maximum drop heights are:

  • Friable coal: 1.0 to 1.5 m [3.3 to 4.9 ft]
  • Calcined coke or carbon products: 0.5 to 1.0 m [1.6 to 3.3 ft]
  • Pelletised agricultural or chemical products: 0.3 to 0.8 m [1.0 to 2.6 ft]
  • Sintered ore: 1.0 to 2.0 m [3.3 to 6.6 ft]

4. Skirting and Load Containment

4.1 Skirtboard Length and Geometry

Set the minimum skirtboard length to 1.2 m per 1.0 m/s [2 ft per 100 fpm] of belt speed, plus 0.9 m [3 ft]. Increase the speed term to 1.8 m per 1.0 m/s [3 ft per 100 fpm] where airflow through the transfer exceeds 0.5 m3/s [1,000 ft3/min], or for aerated or near-spherical bulk solids.

Set the clear distance between steel skirtboards to typically two-thirds of the belt width.

Diverge the skirtboards in the direction of belt travel to reduce material wedging, with a lateral taper of 10 to 15 mm per metre [1/8 to 3/16 in per ft] of skirtboard length.

4.2 Internal Wear Liners

Wear liners (e.g. AR steel, ceramic or CCO) resist bulk material pressure and isolate the external skirt seals from abrasion.

Keep the bottom edge of the wear liner clear of the belt, with a diverging gap of 10 mm [3/8 in] at the tail end, increasing to 19 mm [3/4 in] at the exit end, to release entrapped material.

Set the internal face vertical or angled outward. An inward-angled liner creates a continuous pinch point that traps bulk material and causes localised belt cover wear.

Support the skirted zone with continuous impact beds or close-pitch impact idlers to maintain the liner clearances and reduce material entrapment.

4.3 Skirt Seals

Specify a seal that wears before the belt top cover. A seal softer than the cover, typically 45 to 55 Shore A, achieves this. A seal compound with a lower elastic modulus than the cover also achieves it at higher hardness.

Do not use scrapped conveyor belting for skirting. Exposed fabric plies trap abrasive particles, causing longitudinal belt cover wear.

For high-dust applications, specify a dual-lip seal. The primary lip contains bulk material, while the secondary lip lies flat on the belt edge to capture particulate emissions.

Transfer points are the main source of fugitive dust, as falling material displaces air and the turbulent updraft carries fines out through skirt gaps. Control dust by wet suppression, dry extraction or enclosure.

6.2 Dust Extraction

Where water addition is not permitted (e.g. cement clinker, grain or water-reactive chemicals), extract dust with a dry collection system such as a baghouse or cartridge collector.

A centrifugal exhaust fan draws air from the transfer chute to hold a slight negative pressure inside the skirted enclosure, so ambient air leaks inward through the skirt seal gaps and dust does not escape outward.

Size the exhaust fan on the sum of air displaced by the incoming material, air induced by the falling stream, air induced by the moving belt and air from any upstream crusher discharging into the chute. Exhaust a minimum of 0.54 m3/s per metre of belt width [350 ft3/min per ft] at belt speeds up to 1.0 m/s [200 fpm], and 0.77 m3/s per metre [500 ft3/min per ft] above 1.0 m/s [200 fpm]. Increase these rates 1.5 to 2.0 times for dry, dusty material.

Design for an inward air velocity of 0.75 to 1.0 m/s [150 to 200 fpm] at every open gap, crack or unsealed skirt edge in the loading zone. An oversized fan raises the capture velocity above 5.0 m/s [1,000 fpm], drawing saleable product off the belt and plugging the filter bags.

6.3 Wind Guards and Conveyor Enclosures

Crosswind accelerating over the troughed belt profile lowers the pressure above the belt and generates aerodynamic lift. Lift can raise an empty or lightly loaded belt out of the idler trough and, at sufficient wind speed, displace it from the conveyor structure. Provide wind protection where sustained crosswind exceeds 20 m/s [45 mph].

7. Conveyor-to-Conveyor Speed and Direction Alignment

The interface between the discharging and receiving conveyors imposes two mechanical demands on the receiving belt: a re-acceleration tension load, and a lateral momentum correction where the two conveyors are not parallel.

7.1 Re-Acceleration Loading on the Receiving Belt

Where a rock box or dead-stop transfer is used, bulk material arrives at the receiving belt with near-zero forward velocity. The receiving belt accelerates the material to belt speed through friction between the belt top cover and the material, adding tension to the carrying strand and wearing the cover while the material slips.

Engineering Takeaway: Tension depends on mass flow and the speed deficit at loading, not on skirt length or friction coefficient. Add tension to the effective tension when sizing the drive and checking the belt rating. Velocity-matched chutes reduce both tension and the slip that wears the top cover.
