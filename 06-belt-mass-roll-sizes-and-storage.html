1. Belt Mass, Roll Sizes and Storage

Belt mass and roll dimensions determine structural loads, drive inertia, lifting equipment and transport configuration. Storage conditions and inspection determine whether a stored belt is fit to install.

2. Belt Mass and Thickness

The cover mass factor is numerically equal to the specific gravity of the cover compound, typically 1.10 to 1.15 kg/m2 per mm [5.7 to 6.0 lb/ft2 per in] for standard grades. Fire-resistant and oil-resistant compounds are denser.

Engineering Takeaway: Total belt thickness governs roll diameter and therefore transport height. For a given belt length, roll cross-sectional area is proportional to G, so cover thickness added for wear life reduces the belt length that fits within a transport height limit.

Engineering Takeaway: On fabric belts, cover mass can exceed carcass mass, so cover thickness and compound specific gravity govern belt mass. A fire-resistant above-ground cover compound is approximately 26% heavier per unit thickness than a general purpose compound.

Note: Add the spool or core tare mass, obtained from the manufacturer, to the total belt mass to determine shipping mass.

Engineering Takeaway: Belt mass is the belt mass input to idler loads, sag tension, effective tension and drive inertia. The minimum belt tension for sag control on the return run is directly proportional to belt mass.

3. Roll Dimensions

Calculate roll dimensions to confirm the belt meets transport height and width limits.

Note: The core or spool outer diameter is typically 0.30 to 0.60 m [12 to 24 in]. Add the packaging allowance to the calculated diameter. Confirm the minimum core diameter and the packaging allowance with the manufacturer.

Engineering Takeaway: Where d is small relative to D, D increases with the square root of L, so doubling the belt length increases D by approximately 41%. Splitting the belt into shorter rolls reduces D but adds one field splice per additional roll.

Where a circular roll exceeds the transport height limit, wind the belt around two cores to form a racetrack roll of reduced height.

Note: The overall racetrack roll length is C + D.

Engineering Takeaway: A negative centre distance indicates that a circular roll fits within the height limit. Reducing outer diameter increases centre distance, so check the overall roll length against the trailer deck length and the laydown area.

Engineering Takeaway: Confirm the length on a spare or part-used roll before allocating it to a replacement, as the label length may not reflect earlier cuts. A miscount of one wrap introduces an error equal to one mean wrap circumference.

5. Storage

Rubber covers degrade in storage by ozone attack on unsaturated polymer chains, ultraviolet (UV) oxidation and heat ageing. Moisture wicks into exposed cut ends and degrades the carcass. Aramid loses strength under UV exposure. Seal all cut ends before storage.

6. Inspection of Stored Belts

Inspect stored belts at intervals not exceeding 12 months, reduced to 6 months for belts stored outdoors or within 12 months of the end of their storage period.

7. Pre-Installation Acceptance

Do not install a belt that fails any check.
