1. Conveyor Electric Motors

A loaded belt conveyor is a constant-torque load, requiring rated torque at all operating speeds. Motor technology selection determines electrical network demand, physical footprint, and maintenance intervals.

3. Energy Efficiency

Motor efficiency classes follow the International Efficiency (IE) designation in IEC 60034-30-1. Australian minimum energy performance standards (MEPS) under the Greenhouse and Energy Minimum Standards (GEMS) Act 2012 apply to motors supplied in Australia and are set at IE2-equivalent levels for three-phase cage induction motors from 0.73 kW [1 hp] to less than 185 kW [248 hp].

For motors operating 8,000 h per year over a 20-year life, electricity typically accounts for over 90% of the Total Cost of Ownership (TCO), initial capital expenditure for 2% to 5% and maintenance for the remainder.

Example (indicative costs): A 500 kW [670 hp] drive operates 8,000 h per year for 20 years at 0.12 AUD/kWh. A 1% efficiency improvement reduces input power by approximately 500 kW * 0.01 = 5 kW [6.7 hp]. Annual energy reduction is 5 kW * 8,000 h = 40,000 kWh, worth 40,000 kWh * 0.12 AUD/kWh = 4,800 AUD per year, or 96,000 AUD over 20 years.

4. Ingress Protection Ratings

Ingress Protection (IP) ratings, defined by AS 60529 / IEC 60529, classify the degree of protection provided by motor enclosures against solid objects and water.

Fit a rain guard or canopy to vertically mounted motors. IP ratings apply only in the mounting position tested by the manufacturer. This guard reduces the falling water and debris that bypass the enclosure protection.

6. Motor Thermal Safety Factors

Motor thermal limits are governed by the temperature rating of the stator winding insulation, the rotor cage, and bearing lubrication. Winding insulation systems are categorised by maximum continuous operating temperature per IEC 60085.

Table 5: Motor Insulation Classes
Insulation Class Maximum Continuous Operating Temperature deg C [deg F] Application and Specification
Class A105 [221]Unsuitable for industrial bulk-handling equipment. Restrict to legacy or fractional horsepower applications.
Class B130 [266]Specify for standard duty in controlled environments.
Class F155 [311]Standard for industrial motors. Specify Class F insulation with Class B temperature rise.
Class H180 [356]Specify for heavy duty, high ambient temperatures, or heavy starting.
Class N200 [392]Specify for special high-temperature environments.

Per the Arrhenius relationship, insulation life approximately halves for every 10 deg C [18 deg F] the operating temperature exceeds the rating.

TEFC motors driven by VFDs below 30 Hz experience reduced cooling airflow while I^2R losses at rated torque remain constant. VFD Pulse Width Modulation (PWM) introduces high-frequency harmonics, increasing eddy current and core losses. VFD-driven motors run hotter than DOL motors at identical loads. Compensate by oversizing the motor, applying an increased thermal safety factor, or specifying forced ventilation (IC416).

DOL starting draws an inrush current of typically 600% to 800% FLA, producing I^2R heating in the windings and rotor cage of 36 to 64 times the full-load value during acceleration. Derate S1 (continuous) duty cycle motors if subjected to an S4 (intermittent periodic with starting) or S9 (non-periodic load/speed variation) duty cycle to prevent overheating.

Motors operating above 1,000 m [3,300 ft] altitude have reduced cooling capacity due to lower air density. Apply a larger thermal safety factor or derate the motor per the manufacturer's altitude derating data.

7. Motor Duty Cycle Safety Factors

IEC 60034-1 defines motor duty cycles based on load profiles and thermal equilibrium limits. Specify the correct duty cycle to limit insulation degradation under transient operating conditions.

Motors rated for S4 or S9 duty typically differ from S1 continuous-duty motors in one or more of the following:

  • Larger frame size for increased thermal mass.
  • Independent forced ventilation (IC416) instead of a shaft-driven fan.
  • Reinforced rotor cage to withstand thermal-mechanical fatigue.
  • High-rigidity winding end-turn bracing to resist electromechanical shock loads from frequent inrush currents.

S2 (Short-Time)Constant load operation for a designated period, followed by a de-energised rest period sufficient to return to ambient temperature.Specify for diverter gates, isolation valves, and intermittent positioning actuators.

8. Bearing Currents and Mitigation

VFD PWM induces high-frequency common-mode voltages that accumulate on the motor shaft. When these exceed the lubricant's dielectric breakdown threshold, they discharge through the bearings, and repeated discharges cause Electrical Discharge Machining (EDM), resulting in transverse grooves (fluting) across the bearing races and premature bearing failure.

Fit a shaft grounding ring to every VFD-driven conveyor motor. Above 200 kW [268 hp], also fit an insulated non-drive-end (NDE) bearing.

10. Space Heaters

Space heaters are low-wattage resistive elements installed inside the motor frame. When the motor is de-energised, they maintain internal temperatures 5 to 10 deg C [9 to 18 deg F] above ambient, keeping the windings above the dew point and preventing condensation on the winding insulation. This reduces dielectric degradation, surface tracking, and phase-to-earth faults at start-up.

Specify space heaters for drive motors installed outdoors, underground, or in unconditioned facilities, and where relative humidity continuously exceeds 80%, ambient temperature falls below 5 deg C [41 deg F], or diurnal temperature variation exceeds 15 deg C [27 deg F].

Interlock the space heater circuit with the motor controller so that it energises only when the motor is idle. Ratings are typically 50 to 200 W, depending on motor frame size.

Can produce localised core damage (burning or melting of laminations) if fault current is high and protection is slow.Moisture ingress from failed shaft seals, inoperative space heaters, or damaged terminal box gaskets. Mechanical damage to slot liners during rewind. Conductive dust accumulation bridging the insulation creepage distance. Thermal ageing combined with vibration-induced insulation cracking.Electrical: Earth fault relay trip. Insulation resistance below the IEEE 43 minimum, corrected to 40 deg C [104 deg F] (5 MOhm for random-wound windings and windings rated below 1 kV, 100 MOhm for form-wound windings). Online earth leakage current monitoring detects rising leakage before a full fault develops. Visual (post-failure): Arc damage on the stator bore or slot wall, melted laminations at the fault site.Repair or rewind the stator. If core damage is localised, laminations can be etched and re-insulated. If core damage is extensive, stator replacement is required. Verify space heater operation and interlock logic. Inspect and replace shaft seals and terminal box gaskets. Implement routine insulation resistance trending (monthly for critical drives).
Winding Contamination and TrackingConductive or hygroscopic contaminants (coal dust, mineral dust, moisture, oil mist) accumulate on the stator end-turns and slot exits. Surface contamination creates leakage current tracking paths that progressively carbonise the insulation surface.Inadequate motor enclosure IP rating for the environment. Failed shaft seals or cable gland seals allowing dust and moisture ingress. Oil mist from adjacent hydraulic systems or gearbox breathers depositing on the windings. Space heaters non-functional during extended shutdowns.Electrical: Declining insulation resistance on routine testing, particularly after shutdowns or in wet seasons. Polarisation Index (PI) below 2.0 indicates contaminated or moisture-saturated insulation. Visual (inspection): Dust or oily film on end-turns, visible carbon tracking lines on insulation surfaces.Clean windings with approved solvent and dry with controlled low-temperature heating. Apply a Class H insulating varnish or conformal coating after cleaning. Upgrade the motor to a higher IP rating if contamination is recurring. Verify all seals, glands, and drain plugs are intact. Ensure space heaters are connected and operational.

12.3 Rotor Failure Modes

Table 12: Motor Rotor Failure Modes
Failure Mode Description Root Cause Diagnostic Evidence Corrective Action
Broken Rotor Bars (Squirrel Cage Motors)One or more aluminium or copper bars in the squirrel cage rotor fracture, reducing the rotor's ability to carry induced current. The motor continues to run but with increased slip, reduced torque capability, and localised heating in the rotor.Thermal cycling fatigue from frequent starting (high inrush current generates high thermal stress in the rotor bars). Poor manufacturing quality (casting porosity in aluminium die-cast rotors). Mechanical stress from repeated DOL starts on high-inertia conveyor loads.Electrical: Motor Current Signature Analysis (MCSA) detects sideband peaks at (1 +/- 2 * s) * f around the supply frequency, where s is slip and f is supply frequency. These sidebands grow in amplitude as more bars break. Current: Fluctuating or pulsating stator current at twice slip frequency. Vibration: Running speed (1x) vibration with sidebands at pole pass frequency (slip frequency * number of poles). Performance: Increased full-load slip and reduced starting torque.Reduce the number of starts per hour. If frequent starting is unavoidable, specify a motor rated for S4 (intermittent periodic with starting) duty or install a VFD to eliminate high-inrush DOL starts. For critical drives, implement periodic MCSA testing to detect bar cracking before multiple bars fail.
Rotor Eccentricity (Static and Dynamic)The air gap between the rotor and stator is non-uniform. Static eccentricity is a fixed offset (rotor centre displaced from stator centre). Dynamic eccentricity is a rotating offset (rotor centre orbits around the stator centre). Combined eccentricity produces an Unbalanced Magnetic Pull (UMP) that loads the bearings unevenly.Static: Manufacturing tolerance stack-up, bearing wear, or motor frame distortion from soft foot or foundation settlement. Dynamic: Rotor bow, bent shaft, or rotor mass imbalance.Vibration: Elevated 1x running speed vibration (dynamic eccentricity) or twice line frequency vibration (100 Hz on a 50 Hz supply) (static eccentricity). Electrical: MCSA may detect rotor slot harmonics with asymmetric sideband patterns. Air gap measurement (feeler gauge on disassembly): Variation exceeding 10% of nominal air gap.For static eccentricity, verify bearing condition, housing fits, and frame alignment. For dynamic eccentricity, balance the rotor and check for shaft runout. If eccentricity exceeds the motor manufacturer's tolerance (typically 10% of nominal air gap for standard motors), replace the rotor or bearings.

Cooling Fan Failure or Obstruction (TEFC Motors)The external cooling fan is damaged, missing blades, or the fan cowl intake is blocked. Overtemperature from Voltage or Load ImbalanceThe motor runs hot due to an unbalanced three-phase supply voltage or an unbalanced mechanical load. A voltage imbalance creates a negative-sequence current component that generates additional rotor heating. Temperature rise in the hottest phase increases by approximately 2 times the square of the percentage voltage imbalance (18% at 3% imbalance).Unbalanced supply voltage from utility or transformer tap settings. Single-phasing (loss of one supply phase). Unequal cable impedances on long cable runs. Mechanical overload from belt mistracking, seized idlers, or material spillage increasing drag.Temperature: Winding Pt100 RTDs elevated, potentially with one or two phases higher than the third. Performance: Reduced output torque and audible humming or vibration increase.Measure and correct the supply voltage balance at the MCC (target less than 1% voltage imbalance). Investigate and remedy the source of imbalance (transformer taps, cable connections, upstream load distribution). If single-phasing is the cause, verify the phase failure relay or VFD input phase monitoring is functional. For mechanical overload, inspect the belt, idlers, and loading conditions.
