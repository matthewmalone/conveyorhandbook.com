1. Conveyor Starting Methods and Control

A conveyor starting system delivers an acceleration profile that holds peak starting tension near running tension across all material loading conditions.

Select the starting method from conveyor length, drive power, belt carcass and the degree of tension control required.

3. Control Architecture and Network Requirements

3.1 Tension Wave Propagation

Drive torque application initiates a tension wave that propagates at a velocity dependent on belt construction.

Note: E_b * B is the belt axial stiffness (force per unit strain). Use manufacturer stiffness data where available. Typical v_w is 1,000 to 3,000 m/s [3,300 to 9,800 ft/s] for steel cord belts and 250 to 850 m/s [820 to 2,800 ft/s] for EP fabric belts, at the lower end of each range when loaded.

Engineering Takeaway: Wave speed rises with axial stiffness and falls with moving mass, so a steel cord belt carries tension waves faster than a fabric belt, and a loaded belt slower than an empty one.

Example: On a 10 km [6.2 mi] conveyor, a fabric belt at 800 m/s [2,625 ft/s] gives t_transit = 10,000 / 800 = 12.5 s. A steel cord belt at 1,800 m/s [5,906 ft/s] gives t_transit = 10,000 / 1,800 = 5.6 s.

Engineering Takeaway: A control action at the head pulley reaches the tail only after the tension wave travel time. Base breakaway dwell, ramp segment timing and take-up response on this delay.

Bulk material on the carry strand increases the effective mass per unit length and reduces wave velocity. Use the empty belt wave velocity for control loop and Programmable Logic Controller (PLC) scan limits, because it gives the shortest transit time. Use the loaded belt wave velocity for breakaway dwell and hold durations, because it gives the longest transit time, and for stopping calculations of peak dynamic tension.

3.2 Network Latency Requirements

On conveyors over 1 km [3,300 ft], network latencies above 500 ms can cause belt slip by letting the tension wave reach the take-up before the tail winch receives the command.

Long conveyors with active take-ups require deterministic protocols (e.g. PROFINET IRT or EtherCAT) to keep drive communications under 10 ms latency.

Given a fibre delay of approximately 5 microseconds per kilometre [8 microseconds per mile], a 10 km [6.2 mi] conveyor's 100 microsecond round-trip delay is negligible compared to typical 1 to 10 ms PLC and protocol overheads. PLC scan time is the limiting factor, not fibre length.

Limit drive PLC scan time to 10% of the one-way wave transit time.

Example: A 10 km [6.2 mi] steel cord belt at 1,800 m/s [5,906 ft/s] has a 5.6 s transit time and permits a 0.56 s scan. A 1 km [3,300 ft] steel cord belt has a 0.56 s transit time and requires a scan of 56 ms or less.

3.3 Network Redundancy

Configure critical single-conveyor networks with all devices (PLC, VFDs, remote input/output (I/O), instrumentation) in an automatically rerouting fibre optic ring topology so an isolated cable or switch fault does not stop the drives.

Media Redundancy Protocol (MRP, per IEC 62439-2) provides recovery times below 200 ms, which is sufficient for horizontal, incline and most long conveyors.

High-availability Seamless Redundancy (HSR) or Parallel Redundancy Protocol (PRP) (IEC 62439-3) provide zero recovery time by sending duplicate frames over two paths. Specify HSR or PRP for regenerative decline conveyors and configurations where communication interruptions exceeding 50 ms would cause loss of control.

4. Startup Algorithms and Sequencing

Common practice for VFD-controlled conveyors is a six-phase start: pre-magnetisation, a zero-speed breakaway dwell, a three-segment S-curve velocity ramp and post-ramp tension stabilisation. The S-curve controls jerk (the rate of change of acceleration) to avoid abrupt tension changes that damage splices and destabilise take-ups.

4.1 Pre-Magnetisation Phase (Flux Build-Up)

Establish stator flux before shaft rotation by commanding the VFD to inject DC current into the stator. Initiating rotation before flux is established causes torque pulsations, transmitting transient dynamic loads into the drive pulley and belt splice.

The pre-magnetisation phase consists of initial flux injection and flux stabilisation stages.

During initial flux injection, the VFD applies DC voltage to the stator windings, generating a stationary magnetic field. Flux build-up rate is governed by the motor's rotor time constant (L_r / R_r, where L_r is the rotor inductance and R_r is the rotor resistance). The VFD ramps the DC current to the magnetising level (typically 40% to 60% of rated current) over a programmable period.

During flux stabilisation, the VFD holds the magnetising current steady to achieve a uniform, steady-state air gap flux distribution.

Factors affecting pre-magnetisation time include the following:

  • Higher capacity motors have longer rotor time constants and require longer flux build-up. A 200 kW [268 hp] Low Voltage (LV) motor requires 0.3 to 0.5 seconds and a 1,000 kW [1,341 hp] LV motor requires 0.8 to 1.2 seconds.
  • MV motors (3,300 V and above) have longer rotor time constants. MV motors above 1,000 kW [1,341 hp] typically require 1.5 to 2.5 seconds.
  • Lower rotor resistance in a cold motor increases the rotor time constant, extending magnetisation time. Set VFD parameters for the worst-case cold start condition.
  • Sensorless vector control relies on accurate flux estimation. Inadequate magnetisation corrupts this model and causes acceleration torque errors. Drives with encoder feedback (closed-loop vector) are less sensitive but still require full magnetisation before rotation.

Rules of Thumb:

  • Standard LV induction motors (up to 500 kW [670 hp] at 415 V or 690 V): 0.3 to 0.8 seconds.
  • Large LV induction motors (500 to 1,500 kW [670 to 2,000 hp] at 690 V): 0.8 to 1.5 seconds.
  • MV induction motors (3,300 to 6,600 V, above 1,000 kW [1,341 hp]): 1.5 to 2.5 seconds.
  • MV synchronous motors (for LCI drives): 2.0 to 3.0 seconds, as the rotor field winding must also be excited and rotor position established before rotation.

Consult VFD manufacturer documentation for specific frame sizes. Conservative magnetisation times have no adverse operational consequence other than a longer start delay.

4.2 Breakaway Dwell (Overcoming Stiction)

Loaded conveyor belts exhibit static friction (stiction) higher than kinetic friction, due to idler seal and grease drag and to belt and material deformation over the idlers while stationary. Applying linear VFD torque ramps can induce drive pulley slip if the effective tension exceeds the traction limit set by the slack-side tension (T2) prior to overcoming stiction. Configure the VFD to ramp torque to a breakaway threshold (typically 110% to 125% of nominal torque) while holding speed at zero.

Note: The 1.0 s term is an empirical margin for torque build-up at the drive.

Engineering Takeaway: The dwell allows the initial stretch wave to propagate the full length of the belt, ensuring progressive stiction release. This formula provides a theoretical minimum based on wave propagation alone. In practice, increase the dwell time by 20% to 50% for belt sag between idlers, then apply the factors below.

Rules of Thumb:

  • Short Plant Conveyors (under 500 m [1,640 ft]): 1.0 to 2.0 seconds.
  • Medium Conveyors (500 m to 3 km [1,640 ft to 1.9 mi]): 2.0 to 5.0 seconds.
  • Long Conveyors (3 to 10 km [1.9 to 6.2 mi]): 5.0 to 10.0 seconds.
  • Very Long Conveyors (above 10 km [6.2 mi]): 10.0 to 15.0 seconds.

Factors affecting dwell time selection include the following:

  • At temperatures below 0 deg C [32 deg F], idler grease viscosity and rubber compound stiffness increase, raising stiction. Increase dwell times by 30% to 50% for sub-zero starts.
  • Set the dwell torque threshold based on the fully loaded condition to account for increased normal force and resulting stiction.
  • Degraded grease in aged bearings increases breakaway resistance. Apply dwell times at the upper end of the range for older systems.
  • Conveyors with vertical curves (concave and convex) require additional time for the tension wave to redistribute around the curve transitions. Add 0.5 to 1.0 seconds per major vertical curve.

4.3 Low-Speed Pre-Tension Stage

Low-modulus fabric and solid woven belts, long conveyors, valley profiles and downhill conveyors hold a large length of slack belt at standstill. The breakaway dwell lets the tension wave reach the tail, but it does not remove this slack. Where dynamic simulation shows low tensions or excessive take-up movement early in the ramp, insert a low-speed pre-tension stage between the breakaway dwell and the lower knee of the S-curve.

During the pre-tension stage, the drives run the belt at a low, constant or gently rising speed, typically 4% to 10% of rated speed. This draws slack belt out of the low-tension zones into the take-up and sets up the tension along the whole belt before the main acceleration begins. On downhill conveyors, it also accelerates the declined section gently before full acceleration torque is applied.

Engineering Takeaway: Belt stretch and slack set the length of the pre-tension stage, not wave transit time, so it lasts far longer than the breakaway dwell.

4.4 S-Curve and Jerk Control

Once stiction is overcome, the VFD executes the S-curve velocity ramp. Do not use a simple linear ramp. The step change from zero to constant acceleration produces theoretically infinite jerk at the transition point, generating the tension transients the S-curve avoids.

Rules of Thumb:

  • Acceleration, plant conveyors: 0.1 to 0.2 m/s2 [0.33 to 0.66 ft/s2].
  • Acceleration, long systems: 0.02 to 0.05 m/s2 [0.07 to 0.16 ft/s2].
  • Jerk: 0.05 to 0.1 m/s3 [0.16 to 0.33 ft/s3].
  • Total ramp time, short conveyors (less than 500 m [1,640 ft]): 10 to 20 seconds.
  • Total ramp time, medium conveyors (500 m to 3 km [1,640 ft to 1.9 mi]): 30 to 60 seconds.
  • Total ramp time, long conveyors (3 to 15 km [1.9 to 9.3 mi]): 90 to 180 seconds.

Jerk above this range induces take-up oscillation and destabilises T2.

The total ramp time is the sum of the three S-curve segments: the lower knee (acceleration ramp-up), the constant acceleration body and the upper knee (acceleration ramp-down). For a symmetrical S-curve, the lower and upper knees are each typically 15% to 25% of the total ramp time, with the constant acceleration segment comprising the balance.

Factors governing rate selection include the following:

  • Limit the acceleration rate so that the dynamic starting tension does not cause the tension ratio T1 / T2 to exceed the friction limit at the drive pulley. Limit peak T1 during the ramp to 115% to 120% of the steady-state running T1 for VFD-controlled starts.
  • Gravity take-ups have a fixed counterweight and rely on vertical displacement to maintain T2. During acceleration, the counterweight descends to absorb belt stretch. If the acceleration rate is too high, the counterweight lags the stretch and its downward acceleration reduces T2, increasing the risk of slip. Winch-type active take-ups can compensate dynamically, but only within their response time. Set the acceleration rate so the take-up system (gravity or active) can maintain the T2 setpoint throughout the ramp.
  • On long systems, the elastic stretch during acceleration can reach 0.1% to 0.3% of the belt length. For a 10 km [6.2 mi] belt, this is 10 to 30 m [33 to 98 ft] of stretch that the take-up absorbs. Set the ramp time long enough for this stretch to develop gradually rather than as a step change.

5. Multi-Motor Load Sharing

If two separate drives power the same belt without load-sharing controls, differences in pulley diameter (due to lagging wear), control system lag or motor slip cause unequal load distribution. Unbalanced loading overloads individual motors and reduces service life.

5.1 Fluid Coupling Load Sharing

Hydrodynamic fluid couplings operate with slip (typically 2% to 3% at rated torque). Their torque-slip characteristic is softer than that of an induction motor, so a speed mismatch between drive pulleys produces a smaller torque imbalance. This provides a passive method of load sharing, less precise than VFD control but requiring no active coordination.

5.2 VFD Master/Follower Load Sharing

One drive (typically the primary drive with the largest wrap angle) operates as the master in closed-loop speed control. The master VFD monitors the encoder, calculates the torque required to follow the S-curve speed profile, and applies it.

Secondary drives operate as followers in torque control mode. The master sends its torque reference signal to the follower VFDs over a deterministic link with an update time of less than 10 ms, dictating the current torque output as a percentage. The follower reproduces that torque percentage irrespective of its own speed. Set an overspeed limit on each follower so a belt slip or break does not cause runaway in torque mode.

Do not run independent speed control loops on two drives powering the same belt. Small differences in effective pulley diameter (due to lagging wear) cause one motor to apply maximum torque while the other attempts to retard the belt.

The drives share load proportionally according to their rated capacity. If pulley diameters vary, the follower motor rotates at a differential speed to maintain the torque share, avoiding mechanical conflict.

5.3 Intermediate Drive Tension Control

Run intermediate (tripper) drives in tension control during the start and in operation:

  • A load cell measures the slack-side tension leaving the tripper drive.
  • The tripper drive adjusts its torque to hold that tension at a target value while the head drives follow the demand speed curve.
  • Fit a clamp that limits tripper torque to the drive rating. Where the target cannot be held without overloading the drive, the tension is allowed to rise.

Check the empty running case. The target tension, plus the empty-belt resistance between the tripper and the head, must exceed the slack-side tension at the head drive. If it does not, the tripper does work the head drive must absorb, and the head drive is forced into regeneration. In that case:

  • Raise the target for empty running, or
  • Limit tripper torque so it never exceeds head drive torque.

Fill-controlled (drain) fluid couplings do this passively. Once the head coupling is full at the end of the start, the tripper couplings cannot transmit more than the head.

5.4 Tail Drive Control

On very long conveyors with a tail drive:

  • Hold the tail drive inactive until belt movement produced by the head drives is detected at the tail.
  • The tail drive then acts as a follower, applying the same torque as the head drives.
  • Slip detection on the tail drive is essential, with an algorithm that reduces torque when slip is detected.

6. Application-Specific Startup Sequences

Complex conveyor geometries such as vertical or horizontal curves and declines cause non-uniform tension during startup that a single S-curve does not control. A mid-ramp speed hold pauses the S-curve at a target speed for a set duration so the belt tension profile redistributes and stabilises before acceleration resumes.

6.1 Starting with a Locked Winch Take-Up

Where the winch is locked during the stop, the take-up tension after the stop is above the running set point. If the winch is released as the next start begins, it pays out belt to bring that tension down and feeds slack into the low-tension zones. Keep the winch brake applied until the drives have drawn the take-up tension back down to the set point:

  1. Sound the pre-start warning.
  2. Energise the conveyor drives.
  3. Energise the winch with the winch brake still applied.
  4. Give the drives a zero speed command.
  5. Release the drive park brakes.
  6. Begin the acceleration ramp.
  7. When the take-up tension falls to the set point, release the winch brake. The winch then pays out or hauls in to hold the set point.

7. Drive Protection and Fault Handling

Program the PLC and VFD with a protection response for each fault type, balancing equipment protection against the consequence of an unplanned shutdown. Hard-trip faults require an immediate shutdown and manual investigation before restart, while managed faults are absorbed by the VFD through ride-through, foldback or auto-restart to keep the belt running or recover without operator intervention.

Earth Fault (EF)Leakage current to earth exceeds a threshold (typically 5% to 20% of rated current, depending on VFD model and cable length).Insulation breakdown in motor windings, cable joints or slip ring assembly (if applicable).Immediate trip.Test motor and cable insulation resistance before re-energising.
Output Phase LossVFD detects a missing phase on the outgoing motor current.Failed contactor, broken motor cable or loose terminal connections.Immediate trip to protect the motor.Physical inspection of cables, contactors and terminals before restart.
Communication / Network LossVFD loses network heartbeat or control signal from the master PLC (e.g. EtherNet/IP, PROFINET, Modbus).Severed network cable, PLC power failure, faulty network switch or electromagnetic interference (EMI).Configurable: immediate trip, ramp to stop, or maintain last known speed. Maintain last known speed only where all safety stops are hardwired independently of the network.Re-establish communication and issue a network fault reset from the PLC.
