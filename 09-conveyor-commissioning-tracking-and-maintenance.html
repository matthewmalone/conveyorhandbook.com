1. Conveyor Commissioning, Tracking and Maintenance

Commissioning verifies structural alignment and the drive, electrical, braking, take-up and safety systems before operation, and records the baselines for condition monitoring.

2.2 The Splice Geometry Check

If structural surveys are within tolerance but the belt runs off at the splice and recovers once it passes, the splice is likely out of square. Cut splice ends square (90 deg) to the true belt centreline, not the moulded rubber edge. Establish the true centreline by measuring belt width at 5 to 10 intervals over a 10 m [33 ft] length, marking the centre points, and striking a longitudinal reference line through those points.

2.3 Post-Commissioning Loaded Settlement Survey

For high-capacity conveyors, conduct a secondary optical elevation and transverse survey after 3 to 6 months of operation at design load to capture foundation settlement and structural deflection under load. For standard-duty conveyors, routine tracking monitoring is sufficient. Re-level supports affected by settlement using structural shims.

3. Belt Installation and Overland Stringing

Stringing a belt over 5 to 20 km [3 to 12 mi] requires staged pulling and tension management.

3.1 The Physics of the Pull (Capstan Effect and Curve Friction)

Pulling tension increases exponentially with wrap angle at each horizontal and convex vertical curve (capstan effect). Without bulk material to hold the belt on the banked idlers, the resultant of pulling tension in a horizontal curve draws the belt against the inner-radius wing rollers. On a multi-curve route, pulling tension can exceed conveyor structural limits before the belt reaches its ultimate tensile strength.

3.2 Sectional Pulling and Staging

The installation sequence is:

  1. String a high-tensile synthetic or steel wire leader cable through the empty structure.
  2. Position a linear belt puller near the head drive or a flat intermediate zone.
  3. Mount the initial roll on a braked or motorised let-off stand at the tail and draw the belt into the structure with the leader cable.
  4. Temporarily join roll ends for sequential pulling. Join fabric belts with temporary mechanical fasteners. Join steel cord belts with bolted pulling clamps.
  5. Position intermediate tugger winches or secondary linear pullers along the route to limit tension accumulation in horizontal curves and reduce belt run-off at the inner side of curves.
  6. Clamp the belt at the permanent splice stations and remove the temporary joints. Release installation tension to allow equalisation across the idlers, then complete the splices.

4. Drive Train Alignment

4.1 Soft-Foot Check

Loosen each mounting bolt individually while a dial indicator measures vertical foot deflection. Correct deflection exceeding 0.05 mm [0.002 in] using stainless-steel shims.

4.2 Alignment Methods

The reverse dial-indicator method is acceptable for drives up to 75 kW [100 hp] operating at or below 1,000 rpm. Use laser shaft alignment above either threshold.

4.3 Alignment Tolerances

Apply the following tolerances to the final cold-set alignment, measured against targets that include thermal growth offsets.

  • Shaft speeds up to 1,500 rpm: angular misalignment not exceeding 0.06 mm per 100 mm [0.0006 in per in] of coupling diameter and parallel offset not exceeding 0.05 mm [0.002 in].
  • Shaft speeds above 1,500 rpm: angular misalignment not exceeding 0.04 mm per 100 mm [0.0004 in per in] of coupling diameter and parallel offset not exceeding 0.03 mm [0.0012 in].

5. Electrical and Control Commissioning

5.1 Motor Insulation Resistance and Polarisation Index

Measure stator winding insulation resistance (IR) before energisation using a direct current (DC) test voltage per IEEE 43. Apply 500 V DC to low-voltage (LV) motors and 500 to 5,000 V DC to medium-voltage (MV) motors rated up to 12 kV, increasing with rated voltage.

Test each phase to earth with the remaining phases earthed where phase leads are separable, otherwise all phases together to earth. Correct readings to 40 deg C [104 deg F]. IEEE 43 minimum 1-minute values are 5 MOhm for random-wound windings and form-wound windings rated below 1 kV, and 100 MOhm for form-wound windings rated 1 kV and above. Dry out and retest a motor reading below minimum.

The polarisation index (PI) is the ratio of the 10-minute to the 1-minute IR reading. A PI below the IEEE 43 minimum of 2.0 for Class B, F, or H insulation indicates compromised insulation. PI may be disregarded where the 1-minute IR exceeds 5,000 MOhm. Record both readings and the winding temperature.

5.2 Earthing and Bonding Continuity

Verify earth continuity from the motor frame, gearbox, pulley bearing housings, structural steel, and conductive guarding to the site earth grid, and measure earth fault loop impedance against site electrical standards. Confirm equipotential bonding across flanged and bolted structural joints, and on long conveyors, earthing at each gantry support and the take-up tower.

5.3 Phase Rotation and Direction Check

Confirm supply phase rotation at the motor terminals and verify motor rotation direction with the motor uncoupled before coupling to the gearbox.

5.4 Variable Frequency Drive Configuration

Verify Variable Frequency Drive (VFD) parameters against the design specification before loaded operation, including motor nameplate data, current and torque limits, acceleration and deceleration ramp profiles, speed clamps, and closed-loop encoder feedback configuration.

5.5 Soft-Starter Ramp Profile

Verify the voltage ramp time and initial torque settings against starting tension calculations. Set the ramp to accelerate the fully loaded belt without stalling and without exceeding the allowable belt and splice starting tension. Record the ramp time and the measured starting current profile.

5.6 Control Logic and Interlock Verification

Functionally test the conveyor control logic before loaded operation: the start sequence, warning systems, permissive interlocks, trip logic for protection devices, and operator interface annunciation. For conveyor trains, verify cascade start and stop sequences against material flow. Start the receiving conveyor before the feeding conveyor, stop the feeding conveyor first, and confirm that a fault stops all upstream conveyors in sequence.

6. Brake and Holdback Commissioning

6.1 Commissioning Tests

Service brake torque sets the deceleration rate, typically 0.1 to 0.3 m/s2 [0.3 to 1.0 ft/s2] for plant conveyors and 0.05 to 0.15 m/s2 [0.16 to 0.49 ft/s2] for long systems.

  • Static holding test: Load the conveyor to 100% design capacity, stop it, and de-energise the drive motor with the service brake applied. The conveyor must remain stationary. Belt creep indicates insufficient brake torque or a defective holdback.
  • Dynamic stopping test: Stop the conveyor from full speed under 100% design load via the service brake, recording stopping time and distance. Measured stopping distance must not exceed the design value (DIN 22101 or equivalent dynamic analysis) by more than 10%. A stopping distance shorter than calculated indicates over-braking, which imposes excessive dynamic tension and risks belt lift-off at concave curves.
  • Holdback test: With the drive motor isolated and the service brake released, attempt to rotate the drive pulley in reverse using a lever bar or reversible inching drive. The holdback must prevent reverse rotation. Record angular movement before engagement, and rectify backlash exceeding manufacturer specifications.

6.2 Brake Response Time Verification

Measure the installed brake application delay for stopping distance calculations. Mount an incremental shaft encoder on the brake rotor or drive pulley. Record the encoder and solenoid de-energisation signals on a common time base with a high-speed data logger (minimum 1 kHz sampling). Trigger the brake at full belt speed. The application delay is the time between solenoid de-energisation and the first measurable reduction in shaft speed. Measured application delay must not exceed the value used in the stopping distance calculation.

6.3 Static and Dynamic Torque Verification

For static torque testing on an empty, de-energised belt, apply the brake and measure spring force using calibrated load cells, or measure the hydraulic release pressure required to disengage the pads. Calculate static torque from the measured spring force, pad friction coefficient, number of friction faces, and effective rotor radius.

For dynamic torque, run at full speed with a known load, trip the VFD, and apply the mechanical brake. Calculate dynamic torque from the encoder-measured deceleration rate, system inertia (belt mass, material mass, rotating component inertia WR2) and the motion resistances, including the slope component, acting during the stop.

Measured dynamic torque must fall within +/- 10% of the design value. Deviations require brake readjustment (spring pre-load, pad clearance, pad material verification) and re-testing.

6.4 Pad-to-Rotor Clearance

Excessive clearance increases actuation time. Measure clearance using feeler gauges at a minimum of four equally spaced points around the rotor circumference (drum brakes) or at both inner and outer pad edges (disc brakes). Set new installations to the manufacturer nominal value, typically 0.5 to 1.5 mm [0.02 to 0.06 in] for calliper disc brakes and 1.0 to 2.0 mm [0.04 to 0.08 in] for drum brakes. Re-check and record after the first 100 hours, as pad bedding wear increases clearance.

8. Pre-Commissioning Safety and Lockout / Tagout (LOTO)

Execute and authorise a formal isolation and lockout protocol before the drive motor is energised for the first time.

8.1 Energy Sources Requiring Isolation

Isolate and verify the de-energisation of all system energy sources before the first energisation walk-down, and re-isolate before any subsequent maintenance.

  • Electrical: main drive motor, auxiliary (inching) drive, take-up winch, plough scrapers, belt cleaner tensioners, lighting circuits, and instrumentation loops.
  • Mechanical stored energy: gravity take-up counterweight (mechanically pinned at the tower, not only held by the winch brake), active winch carriage (pinned to rails), drive-pulley holdback, and any disc or drum brakes under spring pre-load.
  • Hydraulic and pneumatic: brake release circuits, tensioner accumulators, and dust-suppression lines depressurised to 0 kPa [0 psi] and isolated.
  • Gravitational: residual material on an inclined belt, cleared or restrained with belt clamps before work on the belt or drive.

8.2 Testing of Mandatory Safety Systems

Before first energisation, execute and document functional tests for all safety control systems.

  • Pull-wire emergency stops (E-stops): Actuate each pull-wire E-stop segment to verify switch trip and control logic integration, and record station locations in the safety register.
  • Misalignment (belt drift) switches: Manually trip each misalignment switch. For non-latching switches, verify that the control logic latches the fault and requires a manual reset.
  • Slip detectors (zero-speed switches): Verify static function before the run, and calibrate trip setpoints during the running phase. On multi-drive conveyors, verify logic differentiation between global system slip and localised drive slip.

8.3 The Formal LOTO Sequence

  1. Notify affected personnel and issue a permit to work.
  2. Restrict access to guarded zones until isolation verification is complete.
  3. Shut down at the local control panel, then at the Motor Control Centre (MCC).
  4. Rack out the drive breaker and apply a personal danger tag and padlock for every person working on the system.
  5. Mechanically pin the take-up carriage. Do not rely on the winch brake alone.
  6. Attempt to start the drive from every control station, including local, field, and Distributed Control System (DCS) stations, to verify isolation.
  7. Verify a zero-energy state at the motor terminals using certified testing equipment.

8.4 First-Energisation Walk-Down

Before first start, complete a full-length walk-down to verify the removal of tools and debris, securement of idler frames, arming of pull-wire E-stops, and installation of permanent guarding. Run a bump test (a momentary 2 to 3 s start) before any sustained operation.

9. Guarding and Access Commissioning

9.1 Nip-Point Guarding

Nip points at pulley wrap transitions require guarding, including head, tail, drive, snub, bend, and take-up assemblies. Guards must extend a minimum of 1,000 mm [39 in] from the nip point (common practice) or to the manufacturer specification, whichever is greater. Guard panels must resist deflection and comply with the aperture reach-distance limits of AS 4024.1801 / ISO 13857.

9.2 Interlocked Guard Switches

Hinged or removable access panels require interlocked safety switches that halt the drive and inhibit restart until the guard is secured and the interlock reset. Functionally test each switch to verify drive isolation, and record switch location, type, and results in the safety register.

9.3 Walkway and Cross-Over Clearances

Verify walkway, platform and cross-over bridge clearances above the belt and dynamic components. Confirm a minimum clearance of 750 mm [30 in] above the loaded belt profile at cross-overs. Inspect handrails, toeboards, and walking surfaces against AS 1657 / ISO 14122 or regional equivalents.

9.4 Emergency Access and Egress

For long and underground conveyors, verify during the walk-down that egress routes are unobstructed, demarcated, and illuminated. Functionally test emergency lighting. Where walkway continuity is interrupted by vertical curves, bridges, or tunnel restrictions, document alternative egress routes in the emergency response plan.

10. Conveyor Protection Instrumentation

Commission and baseline-test the supplementary instrumentation required on complex installations before handover.

10.1 Blocked Chute Detection

Blocked chute detectors (tilt switches, capacitance probes, microwave radar, or load-cell systems) monitor transfer chutes and surge bins. Simulate a blocked state to verify the upstream feeding conveyor trips within the specified response time. Validate the logic so that a blockage at Transfer Point N trips Conveyor N (the feeding conveyor) without tripping Conveyor N+1 (the receiving conveyor).

10.2 Belt Rip Detection

Embedded conductor loops vulcanised transversely into the carcass at intervals of typically 20 to 50 m [66 to 164 ft] initiate a trip sequence upon detection of a severed loop. Loop spacing sets the maximum rip length before detection. Steel cord belts use electromagnetic cord monitoring to detect broken or displaced longitudinal cords. Verify sensor registration for each loop or cord across the entire belt length during an inching run, and resolve signal anomalies before nominal-speed operation.

10.3 Drive Pulley Temperature Monitoring

Friction between a stalled belt and a rotating lagged pulley can ignite belt covers, lagging, or combustible dust. Infrared sensors or embedded resistance temperature detectors (RTDs) monitor the drive pulley shell. Typical trip thresholds range from 80 to 100 deg C [176 to 212 deg F], depending on lagging type and site fire-risk classification. Functionally test control system signal reception and trip activation at the programmed threshold.

10.4 Belt Scale Verification

Calibrate belt scales (weighers) installed for mass flow monitoring or custody transfer during loaded commissioning using a certified test mass (chain weight or pre-weighed bulk material) passed over the weigh frame at nominal belt speed, and verify the integrated reading against the reference standard. Include calibration certificates and as-commissioned accuracy data in the Commissioning Handover Package.

11. Inching and Barring Drive Procedures

11.1 Purpose of the Inching Drive

An inching drive (an auxiliary electric or hydraulic motor coupled to the primary gearbox via a clutch or overrunning freewheel) drives the belt at 1% to 5% of nominal velocity, typically 0.02 to 0.15 m/s [4 to 30 fpm]. Primary drives equipped with VFD control and closed-loop encoder feedback can execute inching operations without auxiliary drive hardware, provided the main motor has sufficient thermal capacity for prolonged low-velocity operation.

11.2 Inching Applications During Commissioning

  • First-rotation splice inspection: Advance the belt one full cycle to observe splice transit over the head pulley for edge lift or ply separation.
  • Clamp witness-mark inspection: Monitor marks applied during tensioning for residual slip.
  • Idler alignment under partial tension: Identify tracking deviations not evident during static alignment.
  • Carcass damage inspection: Inch the full belt length to flag transport damage, cuts, or cover anomalies before high-velocity operation.

11.3 Inching Drive Safety Rules

  • The primary drive breaker must be isolated and locked out before personnel interaction, unless the primary drive is used for inching, in which case equivalent safety protocols apply.
  • Inching controls must incorporate a hold-to-run (deadman) switch to ensure immediate deceleration upon release.
  • Maintain continuous two-way radio communication between inspection personnel and the operator, and stop inching immediately on loss of communication.
  • Do not use inching to position the belt within the splice zone while personnel are present. The system must be stationary, clamped, and mechanically secured before splice operations.
  • Verify that the auxiliary inching clutch is disengaged before energising the primary drive to prevent mechanical failure of the auxiliary gearbox.

12. Belt Tracking Physics and Manual Tracking

Self-aligning idlers are secondary steering devices and do not replace manual tracking on fixed carrying and return idlers.

12.1 The Rule of Belt Tracking

The belt tracks toward the initial point of roller contact. Skewing the idler introduces a lateral friction component that steers the belt toward the roller's leading edge.

12.2 How to Manually Skew (Knock) an Idler

If the belt tracks left (viewed in the direction of travel), loosen the frame mounting bolts and displace the left side of the preceding idler frames in the direction of travel. This shifts the initial contact point to the right side roller, generating a corrective rightward force.

12.3 Upstream Tracking Corrections

Make tracking corrections across multiple idler frames upstream of the misalignment point. The required upstream distance increases with belt tension and idler pitch. Typical correction distances by belt speed are 3 to 15 m [10 to 50 ft] at 1.0 to 1.5 m/s [200 to 300 fpm], 8 to 20 m [26 to 66 ft] at 2.5 to 3.5 m/s [500 to 700 fpm], and 20 to 50 m [66 to 164 ft] at 5 to 7 m/s [1,000 to 1,400 fpm].

12.4 Tilting or Shimming Idlers

Tilt the troughed carrying idler frame with shims beneath the rear idler footpads to pitch it in the direction of travel. This orientation generates a continuous centralising force (similar to a self-aligning training idler), typically inducing less bottom-cover wear than horizontal skewing.

12.5 Limits of Idler Skewing

Idler skewing increases rolling resistance, power consumption and bottom-cover wear. Do not skew an idler frame more than 2.0 deg from square alignment. A need to skew more than 3 consecutive idlers typically indicates structural deviations, such as misaligned stringers, asymmetrical splices, or unlevelled pulleys. Rectify these primary defects and return the idlers to standard alignment.

12.6 The Influence of Carryback (Dirty Return Idlers)

Before structural adjustments, clean return idlers of material carryback. Accumulation increases localised roller diameter and alters relative surface velocity, inducing asymmetric belt pull. A 10 mm [0.4 in] profile variation is sufficient to cause lateral drift.

12.7 Reversible Conveyors

Do not track reversible systems, such as shuttle or bi-directional yard conveyors, by idler skewing. Align idlers on reversing belts square to the centreline within nominal tolerance. Tracking in these systems requires precise structural levelling, laser alignment, and bi-directional self-aligning training idlers.

13. Belt Turnover Commissioning

13.1 Turnover Geometry Classes

A flat helix (unsupported turnover) has no intermediate support and typically requires a transition length of 15 to 25 times the belt width. A guided helix (supported turnover) uses edge rollers, diabolo rolls, or roller cages to constrain the belt, reducing the transition length to 8 to 15 times the belt width. Commissioning of Mobius, flipped-helix and other specialised geometries is manufacturer-specific and outside the scope of this handbook.

13.2 Commissioning Rules Unique to Turnovers

  • Feed the belt into the turnover on the structural centreline. Align the two idler sets immediately upstream of the entry to a +/- 2.0 mm [+/- 0.08 in] lateral tolerance.
  • Do not skew idlers inside the turnover. Address lateral drift within the turnover by correcting upstream alignment.
  • Verify that edge strain remains within the manufacturer's allowable edge elongation. Resolve excessive differential stretching by transition length analysis rather than tracking adjustments.
  • Commission turnovers under full operating tension. Insufficient tension induces asymmetrical sag and transverse instability.
  • Obtain belt manufacturer approval before commissioning at ambient temperatures below 5 deg C [41 deg F].

13.3 First-Run Acceptance for Turnover Sections

During the initial inching run, monitor both terminal ends of the turnover to verify that the belt edge maintains a lateral excursion of less than +/- 10 mm [+/- 0.4 in] relative to a fixed reference point. Edge contact with the turnover framework, or with guide rollers other than those designed to carry the belt edge, is prohibited. Stop the run on any deviation and re-verify upstream entry alignment.

14. The Commissioning Sequence

Tracking a new belt follows a sequential path in the direction of belt travel.

  1. Baseline. Before energisation, verify terminal pulleys are level and square to the centreline, and all carrying and return idlers are squared and secured.
  2. Empty Return Run. Track the empty return run from the head drive towards the tail pulley, centring the belt entry at the tail pulley.
  3. Empty Carry Run. With the tail pulley centred, track the carry run to centre the belt entry at the head pulley.
  4. Loaded Run. Introduce material incrementally to design capacity, then make a final adjustment of the carry idlers to stabilise loaded tracking.

During the loaded run, monitor the take-up assembly to verify tension stability. For winch take-up systems, verify steady-state tension against the modelled no-slip setpoint, and load cell stability and control system response to a simulated overload condition without triggering winch resonant oscillation. For gravity take-up towers, minor carriage movement is normal. Static locking against guide rails or excessive vertical displacement indicates binding guide rollers, sheave misalignment, incorrect trim-weight estimation, or harmonic resonance from under-tensioning.

15. Multi-Drive Load Sharing Verification

Asymmetric torque distribution causes localised thermal and mechanical drive train stress, and raises local belt tension above design parameters.

15.1 Verification Under Load

Operate the conveyor at 100% design capacity and steady-state speed, recording simultaneous motor current and drive-reported torque for each drive. For dual-drive configurations designed for equal distribution, accept a steady-state load on each drive within +/- 10% of the design share. Imbalance exceeding this tolerance indicates speed reference mismatch, improper droop tuning, unequal pulley diameters, lagging wear altering effective rolling radius, or faulty torque feedback.

15.2 Transient Load Sharing

During a loaded start, monitor drive currents across the acceleration ramp. If transient distribution deviates from the design tolerance, recalibrate the start ramp and load-share control gains.

16. Transfer Point and Chute Commissioning

Commission transfer point components during the loaded run phase.

16.1 Loading Chute Geometry Verification

At each transfer point during the initial loaded run, verify that the material trajectory from the discharge pulley lands within +/- 50 mm [+/- 2 in] of the receiving belt centreline. Off-centre loading causes misalignment that idler skewing does not correct. If off-centre, adjust the chute liner geometry, deflector plate angles, or impact cradle positions before proceeding with the loaded tracking phase.

16.2 Skirtboard Sealing and Gap Setting

Set the rubber seal gap to 3 to 6 mm [0.12 to 0.24 in] above the belt surface. Excessive clearance allows fines to escape and contaminate the return run. Extend skirtboards typically 2.0 to 3.0 times the belt width beyond the final impact point.

16.3 Impact Idlers and Cradles

Install impact idlers (rubber disc rolls) or continuous impact cradles (low-friction slider beds with rubber bars) directly beneath the material trajectory. Set the support surface 5 to 10 mm [0.2 to 0.4 in] below the adjacent carrying idler profile to limit belt sag during impact while avoiding continuous frictional drag under zero-load conditions.

16.4 Dust Containment and Suppression

Verify the following during commissioning.

  • Passive containment: Verify curtains, baffles, and stilling zones are intact and correctly positioned, and dust curtains hang freely without excessive drag.
  • Active suppression: Ensure spray nozzles target the material stream before receiving belt contact. Verify suppression logic is interlocked with belt motion and load sensors. Record baseline water flow rates and nozzle pressures.
  • Visual assessment: Document any visible plumes breaching the chute boundary during the first hour at 100% design capacity.

17. Cold-Weather and Hot-Weather Commissioning

17.1 Cold-Weather Commissioning

Ice accumulation on drive-pulley lagging reduces the friction coefficient, increasing start-up slip and thermal damage risks. Before the first cold start, inspect and dry the lagging, and if necessary heat it with radiant heaters. Specify cold-resistant cover compounds where the minimum ambient temperature is below the standard grade rating, and confirm the rating against manufacturer data.

ISO VG 220 gear oil at minus 20 deg C [minus 4 deg F] can overload the input coupling on start-up. Pre-heat gearbox sumps with immersion heaters before the first cold start. Specify synthetic polyalphaolefin (PAO) oils rated for continuous service down to minus 40 deg C [minus 40 deg F] for low-temperature environments. Mineral oils are typically unsuitable below minus 20 deg C [minus 4 deg F] regardless of pour-point additives.

A 500 m [1,640 ft] steel truss contracts by approximately 300 mm [12 in] between 25 deg C and minus 25 deg C [77 deg F and minus 13 deg F], based on a coefficient of thermal expansion of 12 * 10^-6 per deg C [6.7 * 10^-6 per deg F]. Verify thermal-expansion joints are free to move before cold start commissioning.

17.2 Hot-Weather Commissioning

Steel structure in direct sun can reach 60 to 70 deg C [140 to 158 deg F]. A temperature rise of 40 to 50 deg C [72 to 90 deg F] above the morning baseline expands a 500 m [1,640 ft] truss by approximately 240 to 300 mm [9.5 to 12 in]. Log final alignment surveys with ambient temperature so expansion effects can be separated from true settlement at the 3 to 6 month resurvey.

Rubber covers soften above 50 deg C [122 deg F]. Reduce scraper tension accordingly. Specify additional take-up travel for belt thermal expansion beyond the calculated permanent-elongation allowance.

18. No-Load and Loaded Test-Run Acceptance Criteria

Record the following quantitative baselines as the minimum acceptance dataset for future condition monitoring.

18.1 Motor Amperage Baseline

Record steady-state motor current at the MCC under three conditions: no-load (empty), 50% loaded, and 100% loaded, each after at least 30 minutes of continuous operation. Accept no-load current within +/- 20% of the modelled design value. A higher reading indicates excessive parasitic drag. Investigate it before loading. Accept 100% loaded current within +/- 10% of the design calculation to DIN 22101.

18.2 Bearing Temperature Limits

Monitor plummer-block and pulley bearing temperatures during the first 8 hours of loaded running. Stop the conveyor and investigate the bearing where the stabilised bearing shell temperature exceeds either limit:

  • Temperature rise: ambient plus 40 deg C [ambient plus 72 deg F].
  • Absolute ceiling: 80 deg C [176 deg F].

Note: Where ambient exceeds 40 deg C [104 deg F], the absolute ceiling governs. A reading above the absolute ceiling that meets the rise limit requires a review of the lubricant specification.

18.3 Broadband Vibration Limits per ISO 20816-3

Measure broadband velocity root mean square (RMS) vibration on drive motors, gearboxes, and pulley bearings using a calibrated accelerometer mounted to the bearing housing in horizontal, vertical, and axial directions, at steady-state operating speed and 100% design load after a minimum 30-minute run time.

ISO 20816-3 (formerly ISO 10816-3) applies to industrial machines above 15 kW [20 hp] operating between 120 and 30,000 rpm. Measure over the 10 to 1,000 Hz band, or 2 to 1,000 Hz for machines below 600 rpm. Zone limits apply to radial measurements, and to axial measurements only at thrust bearings. Classify the support as rigid where the lowest natural frequency of the machine and support system in the direction of measurement exceeds the running frequency by at least 25%, and otherwise as flexible.

  • Zone B (acceptable for unrestricted long-term operation): A machine entering service in Zone B within the acceptance value is not rejected. Flag the asset for shortened inspection intervals.
  • Zone C (restricted operation): Unsatisfactory for long-term operation. Rectify before handover.
  • Zone D (damage likely): Not released to operations.

For any Zone C or D reading, identify and correct the root cause (imbalance, misalignment, looseness, resonance, or bearing defect), then re-measure to confirm the reading has returned to Zone A or B.

Pulley bearings running below 120 rpm are outside the scope of ISO 20816-3. Assess them against the bearing or pulley manufacturer's limits, and trend them from the commissioning baseline.

18.4 Bearing Defect Frequency Analysis

Broadband RMS can remain in Zone A while early raceway pitting or spalling propagates. Supplement broadband acceptance on critical assets with narrow-band spectral analysis targeting the characteristic bearing race frequencies.

Calculate the following defect frequencies for every commissioned bearing from the manufacturer geometry data and the measured shaft speed, and record them in the commissioning report as narrow-band inspection targets for the life of the machine. Elevated energy at each indicates:

  • Ball Pass Frequency, Outer race (BPFO): outer race spalling, pitting, or brinelling.
  • Ball Pass Frequency, Inner race (BPFI), typically with running-speed sidebands: inner race damage.
  • Ball Spin Frequency (BSF): rolling element flats, spalls, or cracks.
  • Fundamental Train Frequency (FTF, cage frequency): cage wear, cage cracking, or lubrication breakdown.

During commissioning, capture a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) spectrum from each bearing housing in the 0 to 2,000 Hz band. Investigate and rectify before handover where:

  • A discrete peak is present at any of BPFO, BPFI, BSF, or FTF, or their first three harmonics, indicating a manufacturing defect, installation damage (such as brinelling from hammer blows during press-fit), or incorrect preload.
  • The 1X running-speed peak dominates the spectrum and the broadband level is above Zone A, indicating rotor imbalance or a bent shaft.
  • The 2X running-speed peak exceeds 50% of the 1X peak, particularly with elevated axial vibration, indicating coupling misalignment.
  • Sidebands at shaft speed or cage frequency appear around the bearing race frequencies, regardless of overall RMS level.

Add high-frequency envelope detection (demodulation or spike-energy analysis) in the 5 to 20 kHz band for high-tension overland conveyors, long-stroke take-ups, drive trains rated above 500 kW [670 hp], and assets where an unplanned bearing failure would cause a multi-day production outage.

Store all vibration data (broadband RMS, FFT spectra, defect frequencies, and envelope spectra) in the Commissioning Handover Package in a non-proprietary format (e.g. CSV, XML, JSON), with proprietary binary formats as secondary records only. Reject reports consisting only of overall RMS numbers.

18.5 Belt Drift Tolerance

After final loaded tracking, run the belt for at least 30 full revolutions of the loop with no edge drift exceeding 10% of the belt width, and no idler frame left skewed beyond 2.0 deg from square.

18.6 Drive Slip Check

Verify drive slip by comparing drive pulley surface speed to belt surface speed using calibrated encoders or tachometers, or with a transverse chalk index mark across the belt margin and pulley lagging at the point of contact. Relative longitudinal displacement between the marks, or a belt-to-pulley speed difference, exceeding the calculated elastic creep during start-up or a single revolution indicates drive slip and requires take-up tension adjustment.

19. Belt Run-In and Burnishing Period

Rubber belts require a run-in (burnishing) period, typically 50 to 200 operating hours, during which friction, tension, and geometry properties stabilise. Apply the following during run-in.

  • Restrict volumetric throughput to 75% of design capacity for the first 24 hours of loaded operation.
  • Monitor take-up displacement, motor current draw, and bearing temperatures daily during the first week of operation. Expect take-up carriage travel from initial permanent elongation.
  • Monitor for drive slip as cover burnishing reduces the belt-to-drive friction coefficient over the first 20 to 50 operating hours.
  • Inspect splices for edge lift and step separation.
  • Verify torque specifications on all idler frames, pulley hubs, and structural fasteners at 50 operating hours.

20. Belt Cleaner (Scraper) Tensioning and Run-In

20.1 Primary and Secondary Scraper Arrangement

Mount primary scrapers (polyurethane or tungsten-carbide tipped) on the head pulley face immediately below the material trajectory, and secondary scrapers (hardened blades) adjacent to the head pulley on the flat return run.

20.2 Tensioning Philosophy

For tungsten-carbide-tipped primary cleaners, set initial contact force to typically 2.0 to 3.5 N/mm [11 to 20 lbf/in] of blade width. Secondary cleaners require approximately half of the primary force.

20.3 Run-In Schedule

  1. Set initial tension to the minimum specified value.
  2. Use sacrificial rubber scrapers for the first 50 operating hours to limit glazing during early cover burnishing and to remove construction debris, then install and calibrate permanent blades.
  3. Verify and adjust tension at 4, 24, and 100 operating hours.
  4. Reduce tension where drive motor current exceeds the no-scraper baseline by more than 3%, or the cover shows circumferential scoring or heat glazing.

21. Pulley Bearing Lubrication and Grease Compatibility

Pulley bearings, take-up sheaves, and gearbox couplings require a formal lubrication schedule established at commissioning.

21.1 Grease Specification

Pulley plummer-block bearings in the typical conveyor duty range (0.5 to 3 m/s [100 to 600 fpm] belt speed, minus 10 to 80 deg C [14 to 176 deg F] operating temperature) are generally specified with National Lubricating Grease Institute (NLGI) Grade 2 lithium-complex or polyurea grease, an ISO VG 150 to 220 base oil viscosity, and an extreme pressure additive package.

21.2 Compatibility Rule

Do not mix grease types without verification. Lithium-complex and polyurea greases are commonly rated incompatible, and mixing can soften the thickener matrix, causing grease purge and dry-running failure. Tag every lubrication point with the specified grease product and colour, and record the initial charge date.

21.3 Re-Greasing Intervals

Re-greasing frequency is a function of shaft rotational speed, bearing geometry, operating temperature, and environmental contamination. Uncalculated schedules over-grease slow bearings and under-grease fast bearings, causing seal blowout or accelerated race fatigue.

Calculate a base relubrication interval from the bearing speed factor using bearing manufacturer methods, then apply environmental severity multipliers. The intervals below are design guidance only.

For low shaft speeds of 30 to 80 rpm, typical of drive pulleys on conveyors at 1.0 to 2.0 m/s [200 to 400 fpm], clean-condition base intervals for standard plummer-block sizes typically fall between 15,000 and 20,000 hours, or 7,500 to 10,000 hours at the moderate severity factor of 0.50. This is consistent with a semi-annual or annual re-greasing cycle.

For higher shaft speeds of 150 to 250 rpm, such as smaller-diameter pulleys on conveyors at 4.0 to 6.0 m/s [800 to 1,200 fpm], clean-condition base intervals are approximately 5,000 to 7,000 hours, or 2,500 to 3,500 hours at the moderate factor, requiring at least a quarterly cycle. High-speed bearings in severe service may require intervals as short as 6 to 8 weeks.

Temperatures exceeding 100 deg C [212 deg F] indicate a mechanical defect (e.g. over-tensioning, seizure, incorrect specification, or dry-running) and require immediate investigation rather than schedule adjustment.

Labyrinth seals in pulley plummer-block and split housings are scheduled independently from bearing relubrication. Purge each seal through a dedicated seal purge port until fresh grease extrudes visibly from the labyrinth's outboard face, typically 5 to 15 g [0.2 to 0.5 oz] per seal, depending on labyrinth path length and seal stages. Over-purging forces excess grease past the inner seal interface into the bearing housing, causing churning, heat generation, and inboard seal blowout.

22. Permanent Stretch (Creep) and Re-Splicing

Permanent stretch of fabric belting progressively consumes take-up travel. Exhausted travel loses slack-side tension (T2), inducing drive pulley slip, heat, and fire risk if slip detection fails to shut down the drive.

22.1 The Re-Splice Procedure

To restore available travel, isolate the system, secure the belt with rated clamping mechanisms, and reset the take-up carriage to the design minimum clearance. Excise the excess belting and make a new vulcanised splice.

23. Harmonic Belt Flap and Resonance

Belt flap, the vertical oscillation of the belt between idler frames, occurs primarily on the empty return run and indicates dynamic harmonic resonance rather than capacity or tracking faults. Left uncorrected, it accelerates idler bearing failure and fatigues splices.

23.1 The Physics of the Vibrating Span

Every span between idler rolls has a natural frequency, dictated by tension, mass, and idler spacing, and on thick steel-cord belts by belt bending stiffness (EI). Belt flap occurs when an exciter frequency closely matches this natural frequency. Common exciters are an out-of-round (eccentric) idler roll, a stiff vulcanised splice, material carryback built up on a return idler, or aerodynamic windage across an elevated open gantry.

25. Acceptance Certificate and Sign-Off

The Acceptance Certificate formally transfers care and control from the commissioning contractor to operations and initiates the Defects Liability Period (DLP). Handover is incomplete without this signed certificate.

25.1 Practical Completion and Takeover

Practical Completion requires successful execution of all mandatory commissioning tests, loaded test runs, and satisfaction of acceptance criteria. Restrict outstanding defects to agreed punch-list items that permit safe operation. The Takeover Certificate documents the Practical Completion date, punch-list, rectification schedules, and DLP parameters.

25.2 Defects Liability Period

The DLP, typically 12 months from Practical Completion for mechanical conveyor plant, requires the contractor to rectify design, material, or workmanship defects at no cost, and includes the 3 to 6 month loaded settlement resurvey. Out-of-tolerance structural settlement attributable to the contractor's design or workmanship constitutes a rectifiable defect.

25.3 Conditional and Staged Acceptance

If operational requirements necessitate early service entry, grant Conditional Acceptance via a signed defect register. Assign each item an owner, rectification date, and risk assessment validating safe interim operation. Conditional Acceptance maintains contractor DLP obligations. Full Acceptance requires punch-list closure and attachment of the 3 to 6 month resurvey addendum.
