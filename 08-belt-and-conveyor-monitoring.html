1. Belt and Conveyor Monitoring

Monitoring detects degradation early enough to plan repairs and avoid unplanned belt replacement and the associated production downtime.

Installed costs are indicative budget ranges, with lower bounds for single-unit retrofits on short in-plant conveyors and upper bounds for multi-unit or full-length overland installations.

2. Basic Condition Monitoring and Running Sensors

2.1 Pull-Wire (Emergency Stop) Systems

AS/NZS 4024.3610 / AS/NZS 4024.3611 set the requirements for pull-wire emergency stops on conveyors.

A galvanised steel wire rope runs taut along each side of the conveyor, supported on eye-bolts or guide pulleys, and terminates at pull-wire switches. Pulling the rope actuates the switch on that span.

The switch is hard-wired into the conveyor safety circuit, de-energises the drive and, where the risk assessment requires, applies the brakes. Pull-wire switches must latch and require a manual local reset. Switches with broken-wire detection trip when rope tension falls to zero.

Rope approximately 1.0 m [3.3 ft] above the walkway grating. Supports at typically 2 to 3 m [6.6 to 9.8 ft], with the end supports within 300 mm [12 in] of each switch. Emergency stop labels on the rope at intervals not exceeding 30 m [98 ft] where more than one switch is installed. Emergency stop push-buttons within 1.0 m [3.3 ft] reach where the rope cannot be reached.
Settings and ResponseRope typically 3 to 6 mm [0.12 to 0.24 in] diameter, tensioned to typically 50 to 100 N [11 to 22 lbf]. 2.2 Zero-Speed, Under-Speed and Drive Slip Detection

Undetected belt slip is a recognised ignition source for conveyor fires. A drive pulley turning against a stalled or slipping belt generates frictional heat sufficient to ignite the belt cover.

Provide two separate protection functions:

  • Under-speed detection compares belt speed with design speed. It detects a stalled or broken belt and driveline failure.
  • Drive slip detection compares drive pulley surface speed with belt speed. It detects slip at the drive pulley while the belt is still moving, well before belt speed falls to the under-speed trip.

Measure belt speed on a non-driven pulley. Do not use the drive pulley, motor shaft or gearbox shafts as the belt-speed reference, as they continue to rotate when the belt slips or the driveline fails.

Use inductive proximity sensors triggered by steel targets welded or bolted to the outboard end of the pulley shell. With no belt contact, they tolerate dust, mud, moisture and vibration better than optical sensors or friction-wheel tachometers. Use a contact wheel running on the belt only where no suitable non-driven pulley is available, and allow for wheel slip on wet or dirty belts.

For drive slip detection, fit a second sensor to each monitored drive pulley and compare its surface speed with the non-driven pulley sensor in the programmable logic controller (PLC). Fit drive slip detection on critical drives and on conveyors in enclosed or underground installations. A speed difference above the trip setting indicates drive slip or a sensor fault.

Encoders, resolvers and Hall-effect sensors on Variable Frequency Drive (VFD) motor shafts serve drive control only and do not replace independent belt-speed sensors.

Table 2: Under-Speed and Drive Slip Detection Summary
Attribute Detail
Target Failure ModeUnder-speed: stalled or broken belt, driveline failure. Drive slip: belt slipping on the drive pulley.
Applicable Belt TypeAll
Installation LocationNon-driven pulley for belt speed. A second sensor on each monitored drive pulley for drive slip.
Settings and ResponseUnder-speed: alarm at typically 90% and trip at 80% of design speed, bypassed during start-up for the acceleration time. Drive slip: trip at a speed difference of typically 5% to 10% between drive pulley surface and belt, after a short time delay, set above the normal elastic creep at the drive pulley. Select the number of targets to give typically 4 to 10 pulses per second at the minimum operating belt speed.
Detection Lead TimeUnder-speed: instantaneous trip. Drive slip: trip after the set time delay.
Testing and MaintenanceVerify alarm and trip set points at each scheduled function test. Check the sensing gap and target security.
AdvantagesSimple and low cost per unit. Continuous protection against slip and the associated fire hazard. Integrates with standard PLC or relay safety circuits.
LimitationsA seized sensing pulley or a lost target causes a false trip. Contact wheels wear, slip on wet or dirty belts and lose contact if incorrectly tensioned. Optical sensors require regular cleaning in dusty environments. Indicates slip but not its cause.
Indicative Installed Cost$500 to $2,000 per unit

2.4 Blocked Chute Detectors

A transfer chute blocks when the receiving conveyor stops while the feeding conveyor continues to run, or when wet, cohesive or oversize material hangs up in the chute. Install a high-level detector in each transfer chute, interlocked to stop the feeding conveyor. Set the sensor elevation so that the free chute volume above it holds all material discharged during the feeding conveyor's stopping time and coast-down.

Microwave detectors sense material accumulation across the chute without contact.

3. Tramp Metal Detectors and Magnetic Separators

Tramp metal, such as broken excavator teeth or rock bolts, can damage downstream processing equipment. Overband magnets remove ferrous tramp metal, and metal detectors stop the conveyor for conductive metals that magnets cannot remove, such as manganese steel and austenitic stainless steel.

3.1 Magnetic Separators (Overband Magnets)

Electromagnets or permanent magnets suspended over the belt extract ferrous tramp metal from the burden. Select the location as follows.

Table 9: Overband Magnet Installation Locations

Head Pulley (Discharge Trajectory)The magnet sits in front of the discharge trajectory. The burden expands in free fall, giving higher extraction efficiency than over-belt locations.Requires a monorail or overhead crane for servicing. Specify a non-magnetic head pulley shell, as a magnetised shell holds tramp metal and carries it back into the chute.
Over the Troughed BeltThe burden is deep and compactedRequires a larger magnet to extract metal from the trough base, adding capital cost and gantry dead load
Flattening the BeltIdler trough angle reduced to typically 10 to 20 deg to reduce burden depthReduces the belt cross-section at the magnet. Derate the conveyor volumetric capacity to avoid edge spillage.

Specify non-magnetic idler rolls and frames within the magnetic footprint, typically 1.0 to 2.0 m [3.3 to 6.6 ft] upstream and downstream of the magnet centre. Use 304 or 316 stainless steel shells and shafts, or high-density polyethylene (HDPE) or polymer composite rolls. Steel components within the field can be dislodged and damage the magnet or belt.

The field is a hazard to personnel with pacemakers or other implanted medical devices and damages unshielded electronics and mechanical watches. Establish barriered exclusion zones with warning signage, with boundaries set by International Commission on Non-Ionizing Radiation Protection (ICNIRP) occupational exposure guidelines and the applicable national requirements.

3.2 Metal Detectors

Metal detectors emit an electromagnetic field through the belt to detect conductive objects. On detection, the system stops the conveyor and deploys a dye spray or bag marker to locate the object for manual removal.

Fabricate idler frames, stringers and decking within the metal-free zone (MFZ) from structural fibreglass or timber. Insulate structural joints near the detector to break closed conductive loops.

Mechanical belt fasteners trip the detector unless the control system suppresses detection as each splice passes, which leaves that belt length unprotected.

Table 11: Metal Detector Summary
Attribute Detail
Target Failure ModeConductive tramp metal, including manganese steel and stainless steel
Applicable Belt TypeAll with vulcanised splices. Steel cord belts only with detectors that compensate for the cord signal.
Installation LocationUpstream of crushers, high-pressure grinding rolls (HPGRs) and screens, at a distance greater than the conveyor stopping distance, with an MFZ of typically 1.5 to 2.0 m [4.9 to 6.6 ft] in all directions
Settings and ResponseSensitivity set to balance false trips against missed detections. Multi-zone detectors discriminate tramp metal from mineralised ore. Detection stops the conveyor and deploys a marker.
Detection Lead TimeInstantaneous trip
Testing and MaintenanceVerify sensitivity with a calibrated test piece at a set interval. Keep the MFZ clear of stray metal.
AdvantagesDetects non-magnetic conductive metals that magnets cannot extract. Fast trip protection for downstream crushers, HPGRs and screens. Multi-zone detectors reduce false trips from mineralised ore. Markers speed location and recovery.
LimitationsStops the conveyor but does not remove the metal, so manual removal delays production. The MFZ adds structural complexity and cost. Incompatible with mechanical fasteners unless splice signals are suppressed. Requires precise calibration.
Indicative Installed Cost$20,000 to $80,000 per unit

4. Belt Scales / Belt Weighers (Gravimetric Weighing)

Belt scales measure mass flow rate continuously for process control, inventory management and trade settlement. Keep belt tension at the weigh frame stable to limit errors from belt stiffness.

Weigh frames mount either one carrying idler or two to four consecutive idlers on an extended weigh bridge.

Table 12: Belt Scale Summary
Attribute Detail
Target Failure ModeProcess deviation, inventory error, overloading
Applicable Belt TypeAll
Installation LocationStraight, horizontal carrying run away from the tail pulley and downstream of the loading zone once material has settled. Provide three to four idler spacings, typically 3.0 to 4.5 m [10 to 15 ft], of undisturbed belt upstream and downstream. Not on vertical or horizontal curves, within transitions from the loading point or terminal pulleys, or adjacent to gravity or winch take-ups.

LimitationsAccuracy is sensitive to installation quality, belt tension variation, inclination changes, idler misalignment and wind loading. Belt stiffness and drift in troughed sections reduce accuracy, with the greatest effect on single-idler frames. Calibration requires coordination with operations and access to certified reference scales.

Note: Multi-idler frames average load over a longer span, reducing errors from surging, belt stiffness and idler misalignment. This raises accuracy to +/- 0.5% or better, as required for trade-certified custody transfer.

5. 3D Material Profile Scanning (Volumetric Capacity Measurement)

Three-dimensional (3D) laser scanning of the burden profile supports volumetric capacity optimisation, loading and unloading sequencing, and real-time digital twin modelling.

A laser scanner above the carrying belt sweeps a line across the burden surface. Multiplying the cross-sectional area by belt speed gives instantaneous volumetric flow rate (m3/h [ft3/h]), and comparing the area with the design cross-section gives the volumetric fill ratio. Conversion to mass flow requires independent bulk density data.

On systems feeding multiple bins, silos or ships, the scanner tracks the leading and trailing edges of material slugs. The control system predicts their arrival at each discharge point and actuates diverter gates or tripper cars. This reduces material run off the belt end or into the wrong bin during changeover.

Where the fill ratio is consistently low, the digital twin can increase the feed rate from apron feeders, vibrating feeders or variable-speed bin gates. Alternatively, it can reduce belt speed through the VFD to hold the target cross-section at constant mass flow.

An asymmetric profile indicates a misaligned chute, worn skirting or feed segregation, allowing correction before off-centre loading causes mistracking.

Table 13: 3D Material Profile Scanning Summary
Attribute Detail
Target Failure ModeVolumetric overload, asymmetric loading, surges
Applicable Belt TypeAll
Installation LocationAbove the carrying belt downstream of the loading point
Settings and ResponseContinuous volumetric flow, fill ratio and burden shape. Alarm, and reduce or stop feed, when the cross-section exceeds a set maximum, typically 110% of design.
Detection Lead TimeContinuous
Testing and MaintenanceAir purge or regular cleaning of the optics. Check volumetric readings against belt scale mass and measured bulk density.
AdvantagesNon-contact. Detects asymmetric loading, segregation and surges that a belt scale cannot. Enables belt speed control to hold the target cross-section, reducing energy use and wear. Suits retrofit.
LimitationsMeasures volume, not mass, and bulk density varies with moisture and size grading. Dust, water spray and steam foul the optics. Heavy rain and fog scatter the beam. Low-albedo materials such as wet coal weaken the return signal. High capital cost for scanning and processing.
Indicative Installed Cost$60,000 to $200,000 per system

6. Longitudinal Rip Detection

Tramp metal wedged in a transfer chute can cut a longitudinal rip as the drive pulls the belt past it.

6.1 Inductive Sensor Loops (Active)

Conductive sensor loops are embedded in the bottom cover at set intervals and pass a transmitter and receiver mounted downstream of high-risk loading zones. An intact loop couples the signal between them. A rip that severs a loop interrupts the signal and trips the conveyor. Radio frequency identification (RFID) tags beside each loop identify it for location and fault tracking.

Table 14: Inductive Sensor Loop Rip Detection Summary
Attribute Detail
Target Failure ModeLongitudinal belt rip
Applicable Belt TypeSteel cord and fabric
Installation LocationTransmitter and receiver downstream of high-risk loading zones. Loops in the bottom cover at set intervals.
Settings and ResponseLoop spacing typically 50 m [164 ft], which sets the maximum rip length before detection. A severed loop trips the conveyor.
Detection Lead TimeSeconds to minutes, set by loop spacing
Testing and MaintenanceRecord loops damaged by impact or cover wear and replace them during planned belt maintenance
AdvantagesEstablished active rip detection. Unaffected by dust and moisture. Binary loop status limits false trips.
LimitationsLoops are fitted during manufacture or retrofitted by vulcanising, adding belt cost. Impact or cover wear can sever a loop without a rip, disabling that monitoring point. Closer loop spacing shortens the maximum rip length but increases cost. Detects rips only, with no data on wear or carcass condition.
Indicative Installed Cost$50,000 to $150,000 for detector heads, plus a belt premium of $5 to $15 per metre [$1.50 to $4.60 per ft]

7. Real-Time Cover Wear Monitoring

The top belt cover is a sacrificial layer protecting the carcass, and in abrasive duties, its measured wear rate sets belt life and capital forecasts. Continuous measurement reduces the need for manual depth-gauge readings, which require a shutdown and isolation.

7.1 Laser Thickness Mapping (3D Profiling)

A high-speed laser array facing the top cover scans the cover across its width to build a 3D surface map. Software compares the profile with the as-new specification. It identifies asymmetric wear and localised gouging from misaligned chutes or poor material trajectories, allowing chute geometry corrections.

Table 18: Laser Thickness Mapping Summary
Attribute Detail
Target Failure ModeTop cover wear, asymmetric wear, gouging
Applicable Belt TypeAll
Installation LocationFacing the cleaned top cover on the return strand, at a pulley or flat section where the belt is stable. Not on vertical curves, vibrating spans or loaded sections.
Settings and ResponseContinuous full-width surface profile, trended against the as-new profile
Detection Lead TimeContinuous, trending over weeks to months
Testing and MaintenanceAir purge or regular cleaning of the optics
AdvantagesFull-width wear mapping without downtime or belt access. Detects asymmetric wear and gouging missed by point measurements. Supports chute geometry corrections. Builds a digital wear record for lifecycle budgeting.
LimitationsMeasures the surface profile only, not the remaining cover above the carcass. Requires a stable, flat belt section. Dust fouls the optics. High capital cost.
Indicative Installed Cost$80,000 to $250,000 per system

7.2 Ultrasonic Thickness Gauges

Ultrasonic gauges measure the remaining cover above the carcass. A transducer sends an acoustic pulse into the cover that reflects from the steel cords or fabric plies. The echo time of flight gives the cover thickness.

Replace the belt before the top cover falls to typically 2.0 mm [0.08 in] above the carcass. An exposed carcass wicks moisture, causing cord corrosion or ply delamination.

Table 19: Ultrasonic Thickness Gauge Summary
Attribute Detail
Target Failure ModeLoss of cover thickness above the carcass
Applicable Belt TypeAll
Installation LocationHandheld units on the stopped belt. Fixed arrays in contact with the return belt.
Settings and ResponseThickness at discrete transverse positions, trended against the replacement limit
Detection Lead TimeContinuous with fixed arrays. Periodic with handheld units.
Testing and MaintenanceCalibrate against a reference sample of the cover compound. Verify acoustic coupling before each reading.
AdvantagesMeasures remaining thickness above the carcass for replacement scheduling. Handheld units are portable and low cost. Fixed arrays monitor without downtime.
LimitationsHandheld readings need belt access, typically during a shutdown. Accuracy is sensitive to temperature, surface moisture and coupling quality. Fixed arrays measure at discrete positions without full-width mapping.
Indicative Installed Cost$5,000 to $15,000 (handheld), $50,000 to $150,000 (fixed)

8. Steel Cord Scanning (Internal Carcass NDT)

In steel cord (ST) belts, the cords carry the full belt tension, and moisture entering through cover damage corrodes them locally, breaking down cord adhesion and transferring load to adjacent cords. Two non-destructive testing (NDT) methods detect this damage.

8.1 Magnetic Flux Leakage (MFL) Systems

MFL applies to ST belts only, as fabric and solid woven tension members are non-magnetic. A scanner across the belt width saturates the cords with permanent magnets. A broken, corroded or missing cord forces flux to leak from the steel. A coil array logs the longitudinal and transverse position of the anomaly.

Each hot-vulcanised splice has a magnetic signature set by its step pattern. When the rubber between the cords yields in shear, the cords pull apart and the splice elongates and narrows (hourglassing). Tracking this allows planned splice replacement before failure and before hourglassing is visible on the surface.

Table 20: Magnetic Flux Leakage Summary
Attribute Detail

Applicable Belt TypeSteel cord only
Installation LocationAcross the full belt width, typically on the flat return run
Settings and ResponseContinuous full-length scan at operating speed. Resolves splice elongation of typically 1.0 to 2.0 mm [0.04 to 0.08 in].
Detection Lead TimeContinuous, trending over weeks to months
Testing and MaintenanceRecord a baseline scan after installation and after each new splice. Expert calibration to distinguish damage from benign manufacturing and splice anomalies. Regular mechanical maintenance of the scanner.
AdvantagesScans the full belt length at operating speed. Unaffected by dust, moisture and visual obscuration. Detects internal cord damage and splice degradation not visible externally. Established practice for ST belt integrity management.
LimitationsNot applicable to fabric or solid woven belts. High capital cost. Interpretation requires expert calibration. Gives no image of internal rubber condition.
Indicative Installed Cost$150,000 to $500,000 or more

8.2 X-Ray Radiography and Fluoroscopy

Radiography applies to ST belts only, as synthetic tension members are radiolucent and do not contrast with the rubber. An X-ray generator irradiates the moving belt onto a digital line-scan detector, producing a high-contrast image of the cords.

Use radiography for splice quality assurance and quality control (QA/QC), for baseline scans after vulcanising, and to inspect a splice flagged by MFL. Some critical systems use permanent scanners that image splices periodically at reduced speed during maintenance windows.

Verify the rubber gap between adjacent cords against the splice design gap. Reject splices with cord convergence.

10. Automated Belt Cleaner (Scraper) Monitoring

Undetected cleaner failure allows carryback to accumulate on the return run, causing return idler bearing failure, mistracking and spillage.

Position sensors, such as linear potentiometers, measure remaining blade wear, and load cells measure blade pressure. This reduces routine inspection inside chutes.

11. Take-Up Carriage Position and Skew Monitoring

The take-up maintains the belt tension needed for drive traction and tracking. In gravity take-up (GTU) and winch configurations, monitor carriage position and squareness in addition to tension.

String potentiometers, linear encoders or laser distance meters measure carriage travel. Approaching an end stop raises an alarm and stops the conveyor before impact. Excessive travel indicates belt elongation requiring resplicing, drive slip, or failure of a take-up rope or chain.

A GTU carriage can skew in its guides from structural settlement, debris, asymmetric pulley loads or worn guide shoes. A skewed carriage can bind, losing tension control and causing slip or mistracking. Compare the position sensors on each side of the carriage and alarm on divergence before binding occurs.

12. Belt Sag and Catenary Monitoring

Excessive sag increases belt and bulk material flexure resistance, reduces the troughed cross-section, and causes material rollback and spillage in low-tension zones.

Sag is greatest in low-tension zones, such as the tail end of inclined conveyors. Loss of take-up tension, for example from a binding GTU, increases sag there. It can cause rollback and belt instability before speed or drift switches actuate. High sag increases spillage and idler resistance and causes belt bounce at high speed.

Sag monitoring identifies symptoms only. Determine the cause with complementary diagnostics.

Laser rangefinders at mid-span measure upward to the underside of the loaded carrying belt. A gradual increase in sag indicates local tension loss from take-up degradation or belt elongation.

13. Acoustic Idler Bearing Monitoring

Idler bearing failure can cause longitudinal rips and frictional ignition. As raceways spall or lubrication fails, a bearing emits ultrasonic frequencies before it generates enough heat to trigger thermal or fire detection, but manual acoustic inspection is impractical on long conveyors with tens of thousands of bearings.

13.1 Fibre-Optic Distributed Acoustic Sensing (DAS)

A DAS interrogator sends laser pulses along a fibre-optic cable routed the full length of the conveyor. It measures changes in Rayleigh backscatter caused by vibration and acoustic waves acting on the cable, so the fibre becomes a continuous acoustic sensor. Machine learning (ML) algorithms filter broadband operating noise and isolate bearing fault signatures. Optical time-domain reflectometry (OTDR) locates the faulty idler.

14. Thermal and Temperature Monitoring

Distributed temperature sensing (DTS) along a fibre-optic cable gives continuous linear temperature coverage. Fixed infrared (IR) cameras monitor individual equipment. Both complement acoustic and vibration monitoring.

14.1 Distributed Temperature Sensing (DTS)

A DTS interrogator sends laser pulses into the fibre and measures Raman backscatter. The ratio of the anti-Stokes to Stokes components gives the local fibre temperature.

A single fibre can serve DAS and DTS only where the interrogator supports concurrent Raman and Rayleigh measurement. Otherwise, install separate fibres.

15. Drivetrain Condition Monitoring (Gearbox Oil and MCSA)

The motor, coupling, gearbox, low-speed shaft and drive pulley concentrate mechanical failure risk. Micro-electro-mechanical systems (MEMS) vibration sensors monitor bearing condition, and oil condition sensors and motor current signature analysis (MCSA) give complementary indication of drivetrain degradation.

15.1 Real-Time Gearbox Oil Condition Monitoring

Install in-line oil condition sensors on the kidney-loop filtration circuit of each main gearbox. The sensors measure three parameters. Dielectric constant indicates chemical degradation and contamination. Water content is measured in parts per million (ppm). Ferrous wear debris concentration is measured by inductive or magnetic particle counting.

Ferrous debris from early gear-tooth spalling appears in the oil before the damage produces detectable vibration, allowing maintenance planning and parts procurement. Early detection of water ingress from a failed shaft seal or breather allows intervention before water hydrolyses the additive package and corrodes the gear faces.

16. Real-Time Digital Twins and IIoT (Predictive Control)

Calculations to ISO 5048 / DIN 22101 / Conveyor Equipment Manufacturers Association (CEMA) methods set static operating parameters. A digital twin uses Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT) data to simulate the physical state of the conveyor in real time.

16.1 Integrating the IIoT Sensor Network

A digital twin requires continuous, high-resolution data from the following sensors:

  • Motor torque, current and active power (kW [hp]) from the VFD
  • Mass flow rate (t/h [stph]) from belt scales
  • Volumetric flow and burden profile from 3D laser scanners
  • Slack side tension (T2) from shear-pin load cells on the winch or gravity take-up
  • Ambient temperature, which changes cover stiffness and indentation resistance

16.2 Dynamic Parameter Adjustment (Prescriptive Control)

The digital twin compares the model with field data continuously. At reduced mass flow, for example 40% of design capacity, running at full belt speed wastes power and accelerates cover wear. The twin calculates the belt speed that maintains a full cross-section. It checks that tension in concave curves stays above the minimum needed to avoid belt buckling. It then commands the VFD to reduce speed and the winch to adjust tension.

16.3 Validating Low Rolling Resistance (LRR) in Real-Time

Indentation rolling resistance of the bottom cover accounts for up to 60% of power consumption on long overland conveyors, so low rolling resistance (LRR) compounds are specified to reduce energy demand.

Temperature cycling, ultraviolet (UV) exposure and ageing harden the viscoelastic cover over its life and increase indentation loss. The digital twin isolates the power absorbed by indentation by subtracting lift power and the other modelled resistances from the measured drive power. It compares that power with the laboratory value for the new compound, which quantifies energy savings and tracks LRR degradation.

When the cost of the additional energy loss exceeds the amortised cost of belt replacement, the belt has reached its economic end of life.

16.4 Wireless Sensor Networks (LPWAN) and MEMS Vibration

Hard-wiring vibration and temperature sensors along long overland routes, for example 10 km [6.2 mi], requires extensive cabling. Battery-powered MEMS tri-axial accelerometers avoid this cabling. 16.5 Cloud Predictive Analytics and the P-F Curve

Threshold alarms in SCADA systems are reactive. A bearing temperature alarm at 85 deg C [185 deg F] typically actuates after spalling is established, requiring an immediate shutdown.

Cloud analytics apply FFT to high-resolution vibration waveforms. ML models identify the defect frequencies set by bearing geometry: ball pass frequency outer race (BPFO), ball pass frequency inner race (BPFI), fundamental train frequency (FTF) and ball spin frequency (BSF).

The P-F interval is the time between detectable potential failure (P) and functional failure (F). Continuous FFT analysis extends it from hours under temperature alarms to weeks or months, depending on speed, dynamic load and defect type. Lead times are longest on slow, lightly loaded bearings under stable conditions. Heavily loaded head pulleys have shorter P-F intervals and require continuous monitoring rather than reliance on a single remaining useful life (RUL) estimate.

The digital twin updates each degradation curve, issues alerts identifying the defect mechanism and estimates RUL. Parts can then be procured through normal logistics and replacement aligned with planned shutdowns.
