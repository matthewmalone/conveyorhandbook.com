1. Horizontal and Vertical Curves and Transitions

Curve radii and transition distances are determinants of belt, idler, pulley and splice life. Both are fixed by the conveyor profile and terminal geometry during design and are costly to correct after commissioning.

2. Vertical Curves

3. Horizontal Curves

Using a single continuous belt for a directional change eliminates transfer towers and the associated capital cost, dust generation and maintenance. Horizontal curve radii commonly range from 1,000 to 5,000 m [3,300 to 16,400 ft].

3.1 Curve Mechanics

When negotiating a curve, longitudinal belt tension acts to straighten the belt, generating a resultant force directed toward the inside radius. The belt follows the curve by in-plane bending, which stretches the outer edge and relaxes the inner edge. Keep the inner edge in tension at the lowest-tension load case. The tension resultant displaces the belt laterally across the idler rolls until the restoring forces balance the radial load.

The restoring mechanisms are the gravity component from idler banking, the self-centring effect of the troughed profile (the belt climbing a wing roll generates a gravity restoring force) and belt-to-roll friction. Friction varies with moisture and contamination. Do not rely on it as the sole restoring mechanism.

Although centrifugal force acts on the material, the inward component of belt tension predominates at operating speeds. At 5 m/s [1,000 fpm] on a 1,500 m [4,900 ft] radius, centrifugal acceleration is less than 0.02 m/s2 [0.07 ft/s2] and is negligible in the lateral force balance.

3.2 Equilibrium and Radius Selection

Size the radius from a first-pass balance between the inward tension resultant and the outward gravity component from banking.

Note: Rearranged from the equilibrium condition SIN(beta) = T / (R * m * g). Friction and trough self-centring are ignored, so the radius is conservative.

Engineering Takeaway: Required radius is inversely proportional to the sine of the banking angle, so increasing banking from 4 to 8 deg approximately halves it. Outward drift of the loaded belt at minimum tension sets the upper limit on banking.

Evaluate the balance at every idler station through the arc, using the local tension for each load case, and design to the worst-case envelope. Locate curves in low-tension regions of the tension profile where the alignment permits, since required radius is directly proportional to local tension.

3.3 Superelevation of the Carry Strand

To counteract inward tracking, raise the inside of each carry idler set through the curve. Superelevating (banking) the idlers directs a gravity component of the belt and material mass toward the outside of the curve.

Tilt the carry idlers progressively from 0 deg at the tangent point, increasing by 1 to 2 deg per idler frame, and hold the full superelevation (typically 4 to 8 deg) through the constant-radius arc.

3.4 Superelevation of the Return Strand

Bank the return idlers to their own equilibrium requirement. The return strand carries no material, so its tension-to-mass ratio, and therefore its required banking angle, is often equal to or greater than that of the loaded carry strand.

Flat return rolls provide no self-centring, so fit two-roll V-return idlers (typically 10 to 15 deg) through the arc to restore a centring mechanism and retain the banking geometry.

3.5 Governing Load Cases

The empty belt at maximum tension usually governs inward drift, because the tension-to-mass ratio is highest when only belt mass is present. The fully loaded belt at minimum tension governs outward drift over the banked idlers.

Check both against the transient tension envelope from the dynamic starting and stopping analysis, including aborted starts and drift-back, not the steady-state tensions alone.

4. Idler Transitions

The transition distance is the conveyor length between the last fully troughed idler and the flat terminal pulley. The troughed belt edges sit higher than the centre and travel a longer path to reach the pulley.

4.3 Transition Idler Placement Rules of Thumb

The belt leaves the terminal pulley flat and reaches full trough at the first fully troughed carrying idler. Across this distance the edge is stretched above its neutral length and the centre is relaxed. Transition idler sets at intermediate troughing angles hold the belt to a controlled profile, limiting edge strain and centre sag, reducing edge buckling and maintaining the skirt seal.

Limit the gap between successive transition idlers, and between the pulley and the first idler, to the standard carrying idler spacing (typically 1.2 to 1.5 m [4 to 5 ft]). Where the transition distance would leave a larger gap, insert additional intermediate-angle sets to maintain belt support.
