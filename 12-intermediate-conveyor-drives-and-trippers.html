1. Intermediate Conveyor Drives and Trippers

On long conveyors or heavily loaded inclines, cumulative friction and gravitational resistance can raise belt tension above the belt tensile rating.

Either split the system into two independent conveyors with a transfer station, or use a single continuous belt with intermediate drives (carry-side driven trippers, linear boosters, or return-side drives) that inject motor power or braking force and redistribute tension along the belt.

2. Tension Distribution Fundamentals

As the belt traverses the idlers, idler rotating resistance, belt indentation, belt and material flexure, and gravity act against the drive. Tension distribution is non-linear and governed by localised material loading, conveyor geometry, and dynamic operating states.

On horizontal or shallow incline conveyors with a head drive, tension typically starts at its lowest point as the empty belt leaves the drive (T2) and progressively climbs until reaching its peak (T1) at the primary drive pulley. On steep inclines, gravity on the return strand can move the minimum to the tail. Undulating or regenerative decline geometries shift tension maxima and minima away from the primary drive to tail or intermediate stations. Transient conditions including starting acceleration, braking deceleration, and elastic stress-wave propagation generate localised peak tensions that exceed steady-state values and frequently govern component ratings.

Installing an intermediate drive at the conveyor midpoint reduces the head pulley tension (T1). As the belt exits the intermediate drive, its tension drops to a localised T2 value before continuing to the head pulley. On a flat, uniformly loaded conveyor with a midpoint drive, the effective tension carried by each span is approximately halved, and peak tension falls by less than half because the minimum tension (T2) is unchanged.

3. Traction at Return-Side Drives

Return-side intermediate drives operate at the low slack-side tension of the empty return run and are susceptible to slip. Specify an S-drive (dual-pulley) wrap and high-friction lagging for high-power return-side drives. A single high-wrap pulley is acceptable on lower-power return-side drives where the effective tension permits.

5. Intermediate Braking Trippers

Braking systems reduce stopping times and restrain regenerative conveyors against runaway. Applying the required retarding torque at a single location can generate localised tension exceeding the belt's allowable working tension. Intermediate braking trippers distribute braking forces along the conveyor profile, limiting localised tension, and can allow a lower-rated belt class than a single-point braking arrangement.

Do not distribute fixed-torque caliper disc brakes across multiple stations along a conveyor profile. Identical brakes diverge in operation through differential pad wear, which varies with the local tension and retarding torque at each station. As pads wear, the air gap between pad and disc increases, and a brake with a larger air gap takes longer to reach full clamping force.

Brakes with smaller air gaps engage first, absorbing a disproportionate share of the retarding force and generating localised tension spikes. This overload accelerates pad wear at those stations, which changes their air gap, spring force, and response time, so the load share between stations varies unpredictably over the wear cycle. Staggered pad replacement adds further variation unless all calipers are simultaneously overhauled, re-shimmed, and calibrated.

Use regenerative Variable Frequency Drives (VFDs) for retarding at intermediate tripper stations on conveyors requiring multiple braking locations. Conventional mechanical braking remains acceptable where all braking units are concentrated at a single terminal location. Provide mechanical brakes for holding and for stopping on total loss of power, because VFD retarding depends on the electrical supply.

VFDs apply electronic retarding torque without friction wear and with shorter response delay than mechanical brakes. Synchronised via a Programmable Logic Controller (PLC) over a fibre-optic network, each VFD dynamically adjusts torque output to maintain the target tension profile. VFDs require scheduled electrical maintenance, including cooling system servicing and capacitor monitoring, but provide stable, synchronised retarding forces without the progressive divergence inherent to distributed fixed-torque mechanical brakes.

6. Drive Placement Optimisation

Locate an intermediate drive or brake at the chainage that minimises peak tension in the steady-state and dynamic tension profiles.

An intermediate drive or brake divides the system into separate spans, each with its own T2 minimum and T1 peak. The optimal drive location is the chainage minimising the higher of the adjacent span T1 values. On a horizontal conveyor with uniform loading and constant friction, this is approximately the geometric midpoint. On conveyors with variable elevation profiles, concentrated loads, or curves, determine the optimal position iteratively.

On undulating terrain, gradient variations generate multiple local tension minima and maxima. Position the intermediate drive at the downstream end of the section with the greatest cumulative tension rise. Final placement is constrained by physical access, electrical supply routing, and geotechnical foundation suitability.

7. Take-Up System Requirements

Intermediate drives or brakes modify tension distribution and introduce constraints on take-up system design. Maintain minimum belt tension along the entire profile to limit idler sag.

Intermediate drives create additional local tension minima at their respective slack sides. Size the take-up to hold tension above the sag-limit minimum at every local minimum during steady-state and transient operation.

Position the take-up at the point of lowest steady-state tension, typically downstream of the intermediate drive or at the tail pulley. Incorrect positioning induces localised excessive sag or over-tensioning.

Calculate counterweight mass to maintain slack-side tension (T2) at the critical minimum point under worst-case conditions. Intermediate return-side drives require increased counterweight mass to maintain sufficient slack-side tension for traction at the intermediate drive pulley.

Intermediate drives change the distribution of elastic strain between spans, and the total elastic elongation changes with the mean belt tension. Size take-up travel for total elastic elongation under the worst-case tension profile, plus permanent elongation.

8. Belt Tracking and Alignment at Intermediate Drive Stations

Carry-side tripper drives involve multiple direction changes, belt flattening from troughed to flat profiles, and re-troughing after material transfer. Apply tighter mechanical alignment tolerances at tripper stations than on the conveyor run to limit tracking deviations.

Keep self-aligning (tracking) idlers out of the transition zones, the lengths over which the belt changes between the troughed profile and the flat pulley face. Install them at the following locations:

  • Carry side, upstream of the tripper: minimum two sets at 3 m [10 ft] spacing, ending at the last fully troughed idler before the transition to the first direction-change pulley.
  • Carry side, downstream of the tripper: minimum two sets, starting once the belt has fully re-troughed after the final bend pulley.
  • Return run: at least one set upstream and one set downstream of each return-side intermediate drive, clear of the drive pulleys. Where V-return idlers are used, keep the sets outside the length over which the belt flattens onto the pulley.

9. Transient Tension Analysis and Wave Propagation

Transient conditions (starting, stopping, braking, and load fluctuations) induce longitudinal stress waves that propagate along the belt and interact with each drive station.

9.1 Tension Wave Mechanics

Torque variations at the drive or brake pulleys generate tension disturbances that propagate as longitudinal elastic waves through the belt carcass at a finite speed.

Note: E_b * B is the belt axial stiffness (force per unit strain). Use manufacturer stiffness data where available. Typical v_w is 1,000 to 3,000 m/s [3,300 to 9,800 ft/s] for steel cord belts and 250 to 850 m/s [820 to 2,800 ft/s] for EP fabric belts, at the lower end of each range when loaded.

Engineering Takeaway: Wave speed rises with axial stiffness and falls with moving mass, so a steel cord belt carries tension waves faster than a fabric belt, and a loaded belt slower than an empty one.

9.2 Wave Reflection and Superposition

Propagating tension waves reflect at system boundaries. At a fixed boundary (a locked brake or a stalled drive), the wave reflects with the same sign, amplifying local tension amplitude. At a constant-tension boundary (a gravity take-up), the wave reflects with the opposite sign, and tension at the boundary remains constant. Counterweight inertia delays this response, so a gravity take-up acts partly as a fixed boundary during short-duration transients.

Intermediate drive or brake stations act as partial reflection points. The drive or brake inertia and the VFD torque response reflect or absorb part of the wave energy, and the remainder transmits to the adjacent span. The reflected and transmitted components interact with subsequent reflections from other drive stations and from the head and tail boundaries.

Without controlled acceleration, the resulting superposition can produce localised tension peaks of 1.5 to 2.5 times the steady-state operating tension (T1). These transient peaks frequently govern the required belt rating. Synchronise drive response timing to limit constructive wave superposition.

9.3 Controlled Acceleration Profiles

To limit the amplitude of tension waves during starting and stopping, apply VFD torque changes along a controlled acceleration profile rather than a step input. The S-curve (raised cosine or polynomial ramp) is common practice. It limits both the rate of change of belt velocity and the rate of change of acceleration (jerk).

Note: This is an empirical screening estimate, not a closed-form solution. It becomes unbounded as t_ramp approaches zero, whereas a step torque input produces a peak of approximately twice the applied tension change. Use it only to set an initial ramp time for dynamic simulation.

Engineering Takeaway: Longer ramp times permit the tension wave to propagate across the full span and dissipate before the next torque change. On multi-drive conveyors, base the ramp time on wave propagation across the longest individual span, not the total conveyor length. Specify an initial minimum ramp time equal to 2 to 3 times the wave transit time across the longest span (L_span / v_w), pending validation via dynamic simulation.

9.4 Dynamic Simulation Requirements

For conveyors with intermediate drives, analytical approximations of transient tension are insufficient and dynamic simulation is required. The simulation model discretises the belt into elements of length typically equal to the idler spacing, assigns mass, stiffness, and damping properties to each element, and applies time-varying torque inputs at each drive station. The output is a time-history of tension at every point along the belt for each transient event.

Verify by simulation that peak transient tension at every point along the belt remains within the belt's allowable working tension, and that minimum tension remains above the sag limit, under each operating scenario: full load start, empty start, emergency stop, total power failure, power failure at one drive station, and sudden load surge.

The interaction of wave reflections between multiple drive stations produces tensions unpredictable by steady-state methods or single-degree-of-freedom approximations. The simulation results determine the belt rating, the VFD ramp parameters, and the control system timing tolerances.

10. Control Architecture for Multi-Drive Systems

Intermediate drives divide the conveyor into distinct spans and introduce tension reset points in the cumulative tension diagram, requiring coordinated control.

10.1 Master-Follower VFD Architecture

On multi-drive conveyors, a master VFD establishes the speed reference and transmits torque and speed data to follower stations over a communications network. Follower VFDs modulate torque to maintain target tension in their respective spans.

Belt speed at any point is a consequence of the master reference and the elastic response of the belt to distributed torque inputs. Restricting speed regulation to a single control loop avoids competing speed controllers and the resulting oscillatory instability. The master is typically located at the head drive, where elevated belt tension provides stable speed feedback.

10.2 Communication Latency and Synchronisation

Master PLC torque commands to follower VFDs require deterministic synchronisation. If communication latency between the master and followers exceeds the system update interval, followers apply torque asynchronously, inducing dynamic load imbalances.

On a conveyor with intermediate drives spaced 3 to 5 km [1.9 to 3.1 mi] apart, fibre-optic signal propagation delay is negligible (approximately 5 microseconds per kilometre [8 microseconds per mile]). The dominant latency sources are PLC scan time (typically 5 to 20 ms), VFD communication interface processing time (1 to 5 ms), and fieldbus protocol overhead.

Verify total round-trip latency from master command to follower torque response during commissioning using time-stamped event logging. The acceptable latency threshold depends on the belt natural frequency and the acceleration profile. Set the threshold from the dynamic simulation results.

10.3 Torque Distribution and Load Sharing

On a two-drive conveyor, the torque split between drives is determined by the resistance of each span. On a three-drive or four-drive system, the torque distribution becomes a design variable with multiple valid solutions. Two strategies are used:

  • Proportional Resistance Sharing: Torque references are scaled proportional to the calculated steady-state resistance of each respective span. This minimises peak tension (T1) but requires periodic recalibration as component wear and environmental conditions change.
  • Equal Torque Sharing: A uniform torque reference is applied across all drives. This simplifies control architecture and does not require span-by-span resistance calibration but generates unequal tension profiles. Spans with low resistance operate with low slack-side tension (T2), which can induce localised belt sag or tracking instability.

Use proportional resistance sharing for high-power systems where the belt rating is tension-critical. Equal torque sharing is acceptable for lower-power systems where the belt is rated with sufficient margin above the maximum span tension.

10.4 Cascading Tension Profile Resets

On a conveyor with N intermediate drives, the cumulative tension profile contains N+1 spans, each with its own T2 minimum and T1 peak. The tension profile resets at each drive station: the T1 peak of Span K enters the drive pulley, which imparts tractive force to counteract accumulated resistance and delivers the belt to Span K+1 at a reduced T2. This produces a sawtooth tension profile along the conveyor length.

T1 magnitude is determined by the resistance of the respective span, and T2 baseline is determined by the take-up force, the belt self-weight distribution across the return strand, and the torque split between drives. System peak tension (T1_max) is governed by the span with the highest individual resistance, not by the total conveyor resistance.

Each additional intermediate drive reduces peak tension only where it splits or shortens the span with the highest resistance. Justify each additional drive station by the belt rating cost saving against the capital and operational cost of the additional mechanical and electrical infrastructure.

10.5 Fault Tolerance and Drive Trip Scenarios

If one intermediate drive in a multi-drive system trips (loses power or faults), its span and the adjacent span merge into a single, longer span with no intermediate tension reset. Belt tension in this combined span rises, and the remaining drives absorb the redistributed effective tension.

Provide automated fault response logic for every drive trip mode. On a trip, reduce the feed rate to limit tension in the merged span, and initiate a controlled shutdown if the resulting tension exceeds the allowable working tension.

Verify the belt rating for both the normal operating case and the worst-case N-1 drive trip scenario. The N-1 drive trip case frequently governs the minimum belt rating.

11. Belt Cost Considerations

On long conveyor systems, the belt is often the largest single capital expense. A single-drive conveyor accumulates tension continuously along the belt profile and requires a higher-rated belt to withstand the peak tension. This increases component specifications and costs throughout the system.

Higher-rated belts require larger pulleys to limit carcass fatigue, which in turn require increased shaft diameters and higher-capacity spherical roller bearings. Higher-rated belts require extended transition distances and longer terminal structures, while the increased belt and component mass increases the dead load on terminals and foundations.

Adding an intermediate drive at a suitable location reduces the maximum operating tension. Despite the mechanical cost reductions, intermediate drives are not always justified due to infrastructure requirements for remote power distribution. Installing an intermediate drive at a remote location requires an additional local electrical substation and VFDs.

Multiple drive locations on a single elastic belt present complex dynamic control requirements. If the intermediate drive accelerates faster than the primary head drive, slack belt conditions develop, potentially causing buckling. Budget for a dedicated fibre-optic communication network, master-follower VFD synchronisation, dynamic wave modelling, and specialised PLC programming.

Base the decision to implement an intermediate drive on a techno-economic analysis. Compare the capital savings from downgrading the belt carcass, pulleys, and structural steel against the capital cost of the intermediate drive station, electrical substation, fibre-optic network, and VFDs. The intermediate drive is viable only when mechanical savings exceed the cost of the additional drive station, electrical infrastructure, and ongoing operational maintenance.
